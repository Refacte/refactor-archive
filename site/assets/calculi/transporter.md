# Calculate the missing components

Henry Shin · Refactor · Mechanism calculus · Revised November 5, 2026

What does an ordinary subgroup intersection miss in a comparison?

Begin with: Finite groups, subgroups, left and right multiplication.

Given two group elements, can one be obtained from the other by multiplying on the left and right by elements of specified subgroups? Testing every pair can revisit the same endpoints. A graph search avoids that repetition, but then it must remember how an endpoint was reached. Choosing the right record makes membership witnesses and the ambiguity between witnesses part of the same calculation.




## Specify a finite input and two kinds of edge

Let G be a finite group with explicitly available elements, exact multiplication, inverses, and equality. Let A and B be subgroups given by finite generating lists X and Y; include the inverses in each list.


Fix a starting element g. We want a record with two words, one from each generating list, whose value explains the current vertex. We also want to append a new generator to the end of its word whenever we make a move. Writing the record as v = agb would make a new left multiplication prepend to a. Instead store the inverse of the left multiplier:



```text
v = a⁻¹ g b,       a ∈ A, b ∈ B.
```

Now appending x to a gives (ax)^{−1}gb = x^{−1}v, while appending y to b gives vy. This convention is chosen to make the two records update in the same order as the search; using ordinary left multipliers with the opposite word order would also work.


Use the elements of G as graph vertices. At a vertex v allow two kinds of directed move:



```text
left move x ∈ X:       v ↦ x⁻¹v,
right move y ∈ Y:      v ↦ vy.
```

Both moves have reverses. Left and right moves commute with each other: x^{−1}(vy) = (x^{−1}v)y. Moves on the same side must retain their order.


The starting record is (1, 1). Store one such record when each vertex is first visited, retaining both the generator words and their evaluated group elements. Encountering an already visited vertex then gives two records with the same value. The closed comparison between those records will supply information that a list of visited vertices alone would lose.




## Prove which vertices a search reaches

Every path from g has endpoint a^{−1}gb by the invariant. Conversely, write any a and b as generator words and perform their left moves and right moves. This constructs a path to that endpoint.


The connected component is therefore exactly



```text
A g B = {a⁻¹ g b : a ∈ A, b ∈ B}.
```

This set is called a double coset, but the graph has already supplied its meaning. A breadth-first search lists the component and gives a pair of words certifying membership for each vertex. If a queried vertex is not reached when the queue is exhausted, it is not in the component.


Repeat from unvisited vertices to obtain every component. With G explicitly listed, this inspects at most |G|(|X| + |Y|) directed moves. This is a bound in this input model, not a polynomial-time claim for an arbitrary succinct presentation of a group.




## Read the group carried by a closed comparison

A path with pair (a, b) closes at g precisely when



```text
a⁻¹ g b = g,       equivalently       a = g b g⁻¹.
```

Its first coordinate lies in A ∩ gBg^{−1}, and every element of that intersection supplies one and only one closed pair: for a chosen a, take b = g^{−1}ag.


Following a pair (a, b) by (a′, b′) gives (aa′, bb′). Thus the closed pairs form a group under componentwise multiplication, and taking the first coordinate identifies it with the displayed intersection.


In standard group-action language, A × B acts on G on the right by v · (a, b) = a^{−1}vb. The components are its orbits, and the closed pairs form the stabilizer of g. The inverse in the first coordinate is precisely what makes the composition rule componentwise multiplication in this order.


These are evaluated closed comparisons. Two paths are identified when their evaluated pairs in A × B agree. Imposing only immediate-backtracking relations on graph loops would instead give the graph’s free fundamental group, which generally retains more information.




## Extract all closed comparisons from one search tree

A search from g records one pair p_{v} = (a_{v}, b_{v}) for each reached vertex, with p_{g} = (1, 1). Regard a left step as the pair k = (x, 1) and a right step as k = (1, y). On an edge v → w, the old record p_{w} and the newly obtained record p_{v}k reach the same vertex. To compare them at the starting point, follow the new record and then undo the old one:



```text
p_v k p_w⁻¹.
```

This pair fixes g. All closed pairs are generated by these finitely many pairs. To see why, take any closed path with successive steps k_{1}, …, k_{ℓ} and vertices v_{0} = g, …, v_{ℓ} = g. Insert the recorded paths and their inverses:



```text
k₁ ⋯ k_ℓ
 = (p_{v₀} k₁ p_{v₁}⁻¹)(p_{v₁} k₂ p_{v₂}⁻¹) ⋯ (p_{v_{ℓ−1}} k_ℓ p_{v_ℓ}⁻¹).
```

The middle factors cancel, and the endpoint factors are identities. Every closed pair has a generator-word path, so this proves the claim. Take the first coordinates of these closed pairs to obtain generators of A ∩ gBg^{−1}. Finite closure under multiplication then lists that intersection if a full list is wanted.


These are the Schreier generators for the stabilizer. An edge used to reach a vertex for the first time has p_{w} = p_{v}k, so its comparison is the identity. Only the other edges can supply a nontrivial generator. Changing the search tree changes these witness words, but not the subgroup they generate.




## Compute the two components of a six-element group

Use the permutations of three letters, with products acting rightmost first. Set A = B = {1, (12)}. The component of 1 is {1, (12)}. Its closed-comparison group is all of A, of order 2.


Here is one generator extracted by the search. Write s = (12), and suppose the left move reaches s first, so p_{s} = (s, 1). The right move from 1 reaches that same vertex with record (1, s). Its comparison is



```text
(1, s)(s, 1)⁻¹ = (s, s).
```

The first coordinate s generates the intersection. This collision is useful information, even though the endpoint has already been visited.


Now start at g = (13). Applying or omitting the left and right move gives



```text
g = (13),       (12)g = (132),
g(12) = (123),  (12)g(12) = (23).
```

These are the other four vertices. Conjugating B by g gives {1, (23)}, whose intersection with A is {1}. The two components therefore have different closed-comparison groups. Inspecting only A ∩ B would miss the second component and its different group.




## Count the representing pairs

Each vertex in the component has exactly |A ∩ gBg^{−1}| representing pairs. Indeed, if (a_{0}, b_{0}) represents that vertex, all other representing pairs are uniquely



```text
a = h a_0,       b = g⁻¹ h g b_0,
where h ∈ A ∩ gBg⁻¹.
```

Substitution checks the endpoint, and rearranging equality of two endpoints recovers that unique h. Counting pairs now gives



```text
|AgB| = |A| |B| / |A ∩ gBg⁻¹|.
```

In the example this gives 4/2 = 2 and 4/1 = 4. The graph search, recorded word pairs, and closed-edge comparisons give three checkable outputs from one construction: the components, a membership certificate for each reached vertex, and generators of each component's intersection group. Finiteness and effective group operations are essential to the termination and bounds asserted here.




## Sources and comparison

The orbit search and stabilizer calculation are standard group-action methods. The generator formula is Schreier’s lemma; see Peter Webb’s [notes on G-sets and stabilizer chains](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~webb/GAPfiles/StabilizerTheory.pdf), Algorithm 0.1.3 and Theorem 0.1.4. The double-coset formula above is orbit–stabilizer for the displayed action of A × B. The two-word records make that action and its certificates explicit.


Compare [Stallings folding](https://refactor.press/refactors/words-to-machine.html), where labels are read in a free group and a finite graph can represent an infinite subgroup. Here the search ranges over the elements of the finite input group; its termination comes from that finite state space.
