Purity, Prestige, and Pettiness
How pure mathematicians turn taste into rank
Henry Shin | 9 November 2026
https://refactor.press/essays/purity-prestige-and-pettiness

A mathematician interrupts an ordinary exchange to demonstrate a precision nobody needed. Another announces that a colleague's result is trivial before checking whether the hypotheses agree. A department regards teaching as a chore beneath its best people. A numerical method becomes respectable when its useful content has been translated into the vocabulary of a prestigious specialty. Individually, these can look like small irritations. Together, they reveal a culture in which intellectual rank is asserted much more readily than it is earned.

Gian-Carlo Rota was unusually blunt about the atmosphere. “They're snobs,” he said of mathematicians in a 1998 interview. His examples and judgments were often sweeping. The documents collected here allow a more exact indictment. They show pure mathematicians using purity, abstraction, difficulty and proximity to celebrated people as substitutes for examining what another person has done. They also preserve objections from numerical analysts, computer scientists, educators, physicists and mathematicians who were tired of the performance. [17]

The target of this essay is the conversion of mathematical taste into social entitlement. A person likes a certain kind of problem, belongs to a certain intellectual lineage, and learns to express that preference as a judgment of everybody else's seriousness. The resulting pettiness can be ostentatious or exquisitely polite. Its characteristic move is to demand deference while declining the work that would justify it.

This matters especially in pure mathematics because the subject's finest promise is that an argument can be examined independently of the speaker's standing. Its everyday institutions repeatedly require judgments that a proof cannot settle. Which problem deserves time? Which explanation deserves attention? Which work counts as deep? Who should receive credit, a position, or an invitation? These judgments are unavoidable. Presenting inherited taste as though it had the compulsory force of a theorem makes them less accountable.

The evidence assembled below ranges from public statements and documented exchanges to memoirs, archival history and interpretive studies. A memoir establishes what its author reports. A polemic exposes an argument and sometimes supplies a record that can be checked. An institutional study can reveal a pattern without identifying anyone's motives. Reading these forms together produces a history of recurring practices. It does not produce a census of the personalities of pure mathematicians.

The criticism should therefore land where the evidence lands. Pure mathematicians who sneer at computation should have to understand the computation. Those who dismiss an idea as obvious should have to perform the reduction. Those who claim intellectual authority over education or another science should have to acquire knowledge of it. The standards are elementary. The embarrassing part is how frequently a profession proud of its rigor needs to be reminded of them.

I. The rank claimed by purity

When purity becomes a title of rank

A mathematical subject can be described by its objects, methods, questions and standards of evidence. Calling it pure does something more ambiguous. The word can identify a direction of inquiry. It can also suggest that contact with practical demands would lower the work, and that the people who accept those demands occupy a lower intellectual station. Once that suggestion takes hold, differences between mathematical activities become a conveniently self-serving hierarchy. The person whose work avoids applications acquires distinction from the avoidance itself.

Hardy's Apology supplies a canonical version of this vocabulary. His distinction between real and trivial mathematics attaches intellectual rank to kinds of work, while his pride in harmlessness gives remoteness from use a moral attraction. His categories include substantial mathematical physics, and the wartime setting matters. Nevertheless, the book makes it remarkably easy for a reader to inherit contempt for useful work alongside admiration for beautiful mathematics. [20]

Halmos's Applied Mathematics Is Bad Mathematics makes the evaluative maneuver unusually visible. The opening qualifies the title, and the conclusion respects the scientific achievements of applied mathematicians. Yet it returns to the adverse judgment by evaluating their discoveries through an artistic conception of mathematical excellence. The applied mathematician is permitted considerable ingenuity, precision and usefulness while a different activity retains the authority to decide what counts as good mathematics. [19]

The objection is to the allocation of authority built into that arrangement. A scientific problem imposes demands that an internally chosen problem may avoid. A model has to represent something. A numerical answer has to meet an accuracy requirement. An algorithm has to finish within available resources. The boundary conditions may be inconvenient, the data incomplete, the relevant regime singular, the most elegant formulation computationally hopeless. These complications are intellectual content. Treating them as contaminants allows a preferred style to win a competition whose rules it wrote for itself.

Harvey Cohn recalls teachers preaching mathematical purity while engineering departments supplied subjects he needed. He interpreted the prestige of abstention from applications as an academic class distinction. Volker Runde later challenged the evaluative associations carried by the adjective itself. Paul Ernest's analysis of purism develops the distinction between describing an unmixed activity and praising an untainted one. Together these writings identify how a label for a field can carry an unsolicited judgment of the people outside it. [56] [62] [82]

The insinuation is petty because it delivers a personal advantage without undertaking a corresponding intellectual burden. If a pure mathematician finds a modelling problem dull, that tells us something about the mathematician's interests. It does not settle whether constructing a reliable model is difficult, original or important. To reach that conclusion, the critic would have to understand the work. Purity offers a way of receiving the social benefit of a verdict while skipping the investigation needed to justify it.

Zeilberger's reply to Halmos rejects the right to turn mathematical labels into ranks. The force of that objection survives his deliberately extravagant rhetoric. The unity of mathematics cannot sensibly be celebrated when an application validates a favorite theory, then forgotten when the people doing the application ask for equal intellectual consideration. A discipline that advertises its eventual usefulness has accepted an obligation to understand the labor by which usefulness is actually obtained. [5]

The objections come from far beyond one numerical school. Dantzig rejects the purported gulf between pure and applied work in his interview with Peter Horner. A memorial account of Bellman recalls hostility to his attacks on mathematical elitism. Kung's memoir describes Rota's alignment with applied mathematics as an escape from pure-mathematical pettiness. These are participant and biographical assessments, each with its own perspective. Their recurrence makes it difficult to dismiss the complaint as the wounded pride of one disgruntled outsider. [12] [54] [10]

Precision performed at someone else's expense

The hierarchy becomes recognizable in small exchanges. Dagan Karp recounts handing a seminar speaker a bag and asking an ordinary conversational question. The speaker responds as though Karp might have confused the person with the backpack, then invokes mathematicians' precision. Karp's criticism is exact. The purported clarification depends on suppressing a meaning everyone already understands. [55]

Such an exchange can always be described as a joke. Its structure still deserves attention. One person makes a cooperative gesture. Another interrupts it to demonstrate membership in a supposedly more exacting intellectual culture. The information gained is negligible. The exchange creates a social asymmetry. The speaker gets to correct, the other person gets corrected, and mathematical precision is made to sponsor the transaction.

Technical discussion permits a more consequential version of the same move. Hassani, Maillard and Zenine describe physicists asking for representations and examples, only to receive rebukes over terminology or examples chosen for their uselessness. Their introduction praises Jean-Louis Verdier for the opposite practice, generous explanation through concrete objects. The comparison gives the criticism an operational meaning. One response equips the questioner to proceed. The other exhibits the respondent's command of the vocabulary while preserving the questioner's dependence. [11]

Consider the chosen point in the definition of an elliptic curve. A careful mathematician can explain which construction requires it, whether the object under discussion already comes with one, and what changes when a choice is made. That explanation may transform a confused conversation into a productive one. Merely announcing that the physicist has no right to use the term leaves almost all the intellectual work untouched. The distinction is real. The grandstanding consists in extracting a status victory from it while withholding its use.

This conduct is especially irritating because its defenders can retreat to a true proposition. Definitions matter. Hypotheses matter. Words sometimes conceal errors. But a correct proposition about mathematics cannot justify every social use of that proposition. The question is what the intervention accomplishes in the particular exchange. An objection can identify a fatal defect, repair an ambiguity, expose a hidden assumption, or simply make another person look foolish. Those effects should be examined rather than bundled together under the prestige of rigor.

Rota's advice to mathematics departments treats the belittling of elementary work and the comparison of fields as practical institutional mistakes. His reflections on teaching differential equations also describe the low standing of an elementary course and the reluctance of colleagues to teach it. A profession that depends on students can still train its members to regard the work of helping those students as an embarrassing interruption. [21] [23]

The resulting pecking order is intellectually shabby. Explaining a basic construction to someone who lacks the vocabulary can require a clearer command of it than repeating the standard definition to initiated colleagues. Choosing an example that reveals the obstruction can require more judgment than naming the theorem that eventually resolves it. The most conspicuous display of sophistication may therefore conceal a refusal to attempt the more demanding communicative task.

The dispute over practical textbooks exposes the same habit. Seymour Lipschutz's defense of affordable Schaum's volumes quotes Rota against contempt for their readers. Lipschutz had an interest in the series and disclosed it. His intervention nevertheless raises a question that prestige alone cannot answer. What helps a student acquire the skill? A textbook's position in an academic hierarchy and its success in teaching a subject are different things to investigate. [76]

Rota's own departmental advice exposes an additional tension. He warns against belittling fields and elementary work, yet also advises public solidarity and private disagreement. Collegial protection can preserve cooperation. It can also make the outsider the last person permitted to learn that a judgment was doubtful. A profession cannot treat public loyalty as a virtue and then assume that its public consensus records nothing but independent intellectual agreement. [21]

Calling it trivial before doing the work

Few mathematical put-downs are as efficient as announcing that another person's result is an immediate consequence of something classical. The remark claims wide knowledge, quick insight and superior perspective at once. It can destroy the audience's interest before the speaker has time to distinguish the hypotheses. It also creates an awkward burden. The person who has just presented the work must now explain why an apparently authoritative dismissal is wrong.

Zeilberger supplies a particularly instructive confession. He publicly identified a result in a talk by Hyman Bass with a familiar combinatorial theorem and enjoyed the audience's admiration. Afterward he noticed that the assumptions differed. Establishing the relationship eventually took two summers of work with Dominique Foata. His account also describes receiving a superficially similar dismissal from Gene Golub, whose numerical interpretation differed from the closed-form problem Zeilberger was studying. [33]

The first episode exposes the central fraud of premature triviality. The correction arrives at the end of the work, while the social reward arrives at the beginning. A suggestion becomes a finding in the audience's memory. Even if the proposed relationship eventually proves correct, the original performance has misrepresented its difficulty and the extent of the critic's understanding. Being right about a destination does not entitle someone to pretend that the road has already been traveled.

Mathematical reduction has a content. The assumptions must match or be bridged. The objects must be translated. Exceptional cases must be handled. If the claim concerns an algorithm, the translation must preserve the relevant resource bound. If it concerns a numerical procedure, stability and accuracy may survive badly or fail altogether. Saying that two things are essentially the same supplies none of this automatically.

Rota recalls a number theorist dismissing Erdős's work as reliance on a few tricks. His response is that even admired theorists repeatedly use a limited repertoire. The dismissive description selects a feature that can be found throughout successful mathematics, then makes it degrading when applied to the wrong person or subject. The issue is the asymmetry of interpretation. One mathematician possesses a powerful method. Another merely has a trick. [22]

A trick can certainly be narrow. So can a theory. The useful question is how far the idea travels, what it makes possible, and which obstructions it reveals. A method stated informally may organize many arguments. A formally elaborate framework may yield little beyond its defining examples. To compare them responsibly requires attention to their actual reach. The hierarchy of vocabulary makes that comparison disappear by announcing its result in advance.

The same distortion affects simplicity. A short proof can be the outcome of a long struggle to find the right object. Once that object exists, later readers can mistake the ease of following the argument for the ease of discovering it. Then the exposition is punished for doing its job. The writer who removes an unnecessary obstruction has made the subject easier to understand, and a status-conscious reader converts that achievement into evidence that there was little to understand in the first place.

Refactor's interest in changing representations bears directly on this problem. An elementary argument earns its force by preserving the original constraints while making a decisive operation available. The relevant achievement can be inspected. A critic who calls the result obvious should be able to show where that operation was previously available and how it resolves the actual problem. Without that account, obviousness remains an expression of confidence.

Grothendieck's reflections on young defenders of purity return to the distinction between an easy argument and an unimportant idea. The translated text available in this collection requires textual care, but its question is pertinent. When a powerful reader can dismiss a new perspective as obvious, who bears the cost of establishing what that reader has failed to see? The question points beyond wounded pride toward control of publication and attention. [30]

II. The work the hierarchy discounts

What the dismissal of computation leaves out

The expression mere computation can conceal almost every interesting feature of a computational problem. It groups together pressing a button, inventing an algorithm, proving its convergence, controlling its error, finding a usable representation, building a reliable implementation and discovering which question ought to be computed. Once these activities have been compressed into a single inferior category, the pure mathematician can dismiss them with impressive economy. The economy is achieved by throwing away the distinctions on which an informed judgment would depend.

Hamming's Numerical Analysis vs. Mathematics belongs to an older argument over the standards by which numerical work should be evaluated. Trefethen's later essay on the definition of numerical analysis makes the problem concrete. He argues that treating the field chiefly as the study of rounding error obscures its central concern with algorithms for continuous problems. Questions of approximation and speed would remain even in a world of exact arithmetic. [4] [93]

This is an elementary point with substantial consequences. A theorem that a quantity exists does not supply a way to obtain it at the needed accuracy. A convergent sequence does not automatically converge at a usable rate. A finite algorithm may still require an impossible amount of time. Two algebraically equivalent formulas can behave very differently on finite-precision machines. These are mathematical distinctions. Their importance grows when someone has to produce an answer instead of merely describing the set in which an answer resides.

The scientific question adds further work. What accuracy is meaningful given the data? Which features of the model should survive discretization? Where does sensitivity make an apparently precise answer misleading? How can the implementation be tested against independent constraints? Which part of the error can be reduced, and which part comes from pretending to know more about the world than the evidence supplies? None of these questions becomes easy because an internally defined problem permits a tidier statement.

The cultural mistake is to recognize intellectual difficulty only after it has been translated into the preferred format. The algorithm may receive esteem once a theorem about it is detached from the computation. The representation may become respectable once its practical motivation is forgotten. The software may be praised as a tool while the ingenuity required to create it disappears from the accounting. Applied work is then allowed to donate problems and techniques to mathematics while being charged an entrance fee for recognition.

Trefethen's memoir offers a personal view of the resulting disconnection, including an undergraduate culture organized around advancing rapidly through prestigious material. Corless's review connects disdain for practical work with disdain for craft, and also objects to numerical communities neglecting symbolic computation. The hierarchy can reproduce itself at the next boundary. A group injured by condescension can still reserve the right to condescend to someone else. [2] [3]

That observation strengthens the criticism of pure-mathematical prestige. Its influence is visible when researchers seek safety by becoming more like the people who dismiss them. The ambition to be admitted to the higher caste can displace the harder task of explaining the value of one's own work. Instead of asking whether an algorithm solves the relevant problem well, a community begins to ask whether its activities look sufficiently like the approved image of mathematics.

The intellectual work that gets called craft

The condescension toward computation often depends on a comforting division of labor. One person has the ideas. Someone else performs the calculation, implements the method, checks the edge cases and makes it usable. The first contribution appears creative by definition. The others are classified as execution. This picture is convenient for the person occupying the first role, especially when the later work reveals defects in the original idea.

In his oral history, Kahan condemns pure mathematicians who look down on applied colleagues. The force of his criticism comes from work whose demands make the hierarchy look absurd. Numerical reliability requires reasoning about representations, machines and operations together. A beautiful abstract formulation can be one input to that reasoning. It still has to survive it. [61]

Hersh's conversation with Ulf Persson is equally direct about the status motive behind disdain for numerical analysis and the desire to stand above engineering. Lax's interview challenges the assumption that applied mathematics is inherently ugly. These accounts describe a professional outlook that treats closeness to use as a reduction in intellectual standing even when that closeness generates the difficult questions. [46] [47]

Suppose a computation fails near a singular regime. It may require a new formulation, an asymptotic argument, a preconditioner, an invariant, a different discretization or a recognition that the model itself has become unreliable. Deciding among these possibilities calls for conceptual judgment. The distinction between thought and execution cannot be assigned according to whether a keyboard or a blackboard happens to be present. A person can manipulate symbols mechanically on either.

Dijkstra's famous reversal tells weaker mathematicians to remain pure, placing demanding programming within applied mathematics. Its deliberately insulting symmetry exposes how little is established by the original hierarchy. Reversing the ranking changes who gets flattered while leaving the need for an actual comparison untouched. Dijkstra's own commitment to formal reasoning also makes it impossible to enlist him in a simple campaign against proof. [87]

The constructive answer is to identify what an implementation has taught its author. Encoding a procedure can reveal an unstated choice, a hidden dependency, an impossible operation or a gap between existence and construction. Testing can expose a regime the derivation ignored. Maintaining software can force distinctions that a one-off calculation evades. A usable system may embody a considerable amount of mathematics whose location makes it easy for academic accounting to overlook.

Wolfram's interviews attack the exclusion of computation and the failure to recognize system design as intellectual work. His commercial interest is part of the setting, and his polemics have their own excesses. Yet the question they put to the profession is concrete. What is lost when researchers refuse to examine a form of work because they have already decided which professional category it belongs to? [91] [92]

Zeilberger's argument for programming and Wilf's account of mathematical experimentation offer complementary answers. Writing a program can change the questions one can ask. Computation can extend mathematical perception, suggest a conjecture, expose a pattern or falsify a plausible claim. The intellectual contribution lies partly in making those encounters possible. Its value cannot be read off from whether the final artifact resembles the sort of paper that already receives prestige. [34] [50]

Abstraction and the debts it forgets

Abstraction becomes powerful when it carries useful features across problems. It becomes an excuse when its user invokes generality to avoid learning what the problem requires. Those possibilities can coexist in the same mathematical language. A category can organize a difficult construction or supply a decorative description of it. A theorem can open a route to calculation or merely relocate the difficulty. The work of deciding which has happened is precisely what status judgments tend to evade.

Von Neumann's discussion of mathematics warns of degeneration when a subject becomes remote from its empirical sources and proliferates into insignificant complexity. Mumford's writings argue for the productive exchange between abstract and concrete work and criticize the separation of pure from applied mathematics. These are challenges to a habit of evaluation that treats increasing internal elaboration as evidence of importance. [48] [51] [52]

The debt can be forgotten at several stages. A physical problem supplies the original object. An experimental observation suggests the phenomenon. A numerical example reveals the obstruction. A practical requirement forces the right definition. Once a mature theory exists, its exposition can begin with the polished definition and make the earlier labor seem like a dispensable prehistory. What was necessary to find the theory is then judged by how redundant it looks after the theory has been found.

The question is also pedagogical. Arnold's polemics return insistently to students who have acquired sophisticated language while lacking command of concrete geometry and physical examples. Hammersley challenges the authority of pure mathematicians to decide what practical mathematical education requires. Both writers are severe, sometimes extravagant critics. Their examples nevertheless direct attention toward a testable educational failure, the ability to reproduce the language of a subject while being unable to use it on an intelligible problem. [9] [26] [88]

Specialized knowledge can become a shelter from that test. A student asks what the machinery does and is told to learn more machinery. A colleague asks for an example and receives another definition. The promised understanding is always one prerequisite farther away. Some subjects genuinely demand long preparation. That fact becomes a convenient cover when the teacher has failed to choose an example, explain the purpose of a construction, or recognize that the requested bridge is missing.

The same discipline that insists on stating hypotheses should be willing to state the conditions under which its explanations count as successful. Can the reader identify the object? Can the reader perform a representative operation? Can the reader explain why the assumptions matter? Can the reader carry the result back to the motivating question? These demands leave plenty of room for advanced mathematics. They deprive empty displays of difficulty of an easy defense.

Rota's distinction between the lived activity of mathematics and its axiomatic presentation helps explain the distortion. The finished ordering of a subject is one intellectual product. Treating that ordering as the entire activity removes discovery, examples, repair, judgment and communication from view. Prestige then attaches to mastery of the finished language, even when the problem at hand calls for one of the activities the language has concealed. [24]

III. When taste acquires an office

The cost of being hard to place

A professional hierarchy becomes consequential when it controls the conditions under which other people can work. At that point the dismissive remark acquires an office, a budget and a vote. Someone's inability to recognize a research direction can become the researcher's inability to obtain the time to develop it. The institution then congratulates itself for having identified the promising people. Its own contribution to making particular careers possible disappears from the account.

Mandelbrot's recollections expose the problem with unusual clarity. In his NOVA interview he describes a senior appointment withdrawn because his future research was considered unpredictable, and a grant initially refused despite five outstanding reviews and one excellent review. These are his accounts of the decisions. Their significance lies in the criterion they bring into view. The work could be admired while the person remained difficult to classify. [90]

Predictability has a legitimate place in planning. It also offers an extremely convenient pretext for rewarding conformity. A committee can prefer someone whose future resembles its members' past, then describe that preference as an assessment of intellectual promise. A recognizable project arrives with a ready-made audience, vocabulary and succession of publishable steps. A new line of work may have to create all three. To penalize it for their initial absence is to charge originality for being original.

Mandelbrot's account of hostility toward mathematical pictures belongs in the same argument. A representation that made phenomena available for investigation could be treated as evidence of intellectual unseriousness. In his interview with Hans Ulrich Obrist, the resistance concerns both visual mathematics and small professional circles writing chiefly for themselves. The issue is who may decide which forms of apprehension count as mathematical work. [89]

The objection to a picture should identify what it fails to establish. Does it conceal a singularity, depend on numerical resolution, or suggest an invariant that disappears under perturbation? Those are questions that can advance the investigation. Contempt for pictures in general merely licenses the speaker to disregard whatever a picture makes visible. The appearance of sophistication then rests on a refusal to examine evidence in an unfamiliar form.

Cartier's account of Bourbaki gives this exclusion an institutional history. He describes resistance to mathematical physics within the group and the conversion of an initially insurgent movement into an establishment. His interview also credits the group's accomplishments. Precisely for that reason, it helps isolate the moment when an effective intellectual programme begins claiming jurisdiction over the intellectual world beyond it. [25]

A powerful programme attracts students because it reveals things. Its danger grows when its adherents treat the programme's omissions as a map of what can safely be neglected. The resulting narrowness can look like ambition. One promises a unified account of mathematics while arranging the definition of mathematics around the work one already knows how to organize. The discarded material becomes peripheral by stipulation.

Mathias's long criticism of Bourbaki concerns logic, itself an exceptionally pure mathematical subject. He argues that authoritative mistakes and contempt helped shape its exclusion. His later reply makes his polemical challenge to Bourbaki's elevated standing explicit. The extended technical and historical case deserves examination on its own terms. The case shows how the rhetoric of mathematical centrality can be directed against another pure specialty. [27] [29]

The exclusions are therefore capable of reproducing at any scale. Once applications have been pushed down the hierarchy, pure mathematicians can rank one another by abstraction, ancestry, institutional address, or proximity to a celebrated programme. Purity offers no stable stopping point. There is always a purer neighbour, or a better-connected interpreter of what purity ought to mean.

Preferences with material consequences

Steingart's archival history of American mathematics locates the pure/applied division in postwar professional institutions. Her discussion of 1946 correspondence includes Householder's admission that he had looked down on applied mathematicians, alongside disagreements over the American Mathematical Society's orientation. The episode puts a historical decision process beside the later memoirs and denunciations. Ideas about mathematical worth were being negotiated through organizations that could give them practical force. [83]

The institutional problem begins when those judgments become difficult to contest from outside the circle that shares them. A funding applicant must write for a panel. A young mathematician needs referees. A department must decide whose work is central enough to teach and whom it will hire to teach it. Every such decision can be defended individually. Their cumulative effect can nevertheless be to make one inherited style appear indispensable and every alternative appear dispensable.

Brian Martin connects professional purity to autonomy, specialization and control over disciplinary evaluation. His argument examines how a field's self-description can defend its authority. A community can present its preferences as the internal demands of knowledge while benefiting from the power to define those demands. [60]

Chang and Fu's genealogy study supplies a different kind of evidence. Their elite network is constructed from paths connecting Fields Medalists, and their analysis concerns concentration and representation under that definition. It leaves the motives of particular evaluators open. Its value for the present argument is the reminder that mathematical eminence has a social structure that can be studied, rather than merely celebrated. [59]

That structure matters when a profession insists that its rankings are transparent consequences of ability. The insistence can make every unsuccessful challenge appear self-discrediting. Whoever questions the hierarchy must, on this account, have failed to understand it. The successful are authorized by success, while the unsuccessful lack the standing to explain how success is allocated. Such a system could make mistakes indefinitely without encountering a critic it is obliged to hear.

A serious mathematical culture should be especially impatient with that circularity. Its practitioners know the difference between establishing a proposition and repeating an assumption in a more impressive vocabulary. The capacity to recognize circular reasoning should survive the walk from the blackboard to the appointments committee.

The rejection and the reason

Editorial selection is where an aesthetic hierarchy can acquire a deceptively neutral vocabulary. A result is insufficiently interesting, too special, too computational, or unsuitable for the audience. Any of these judgments can be defensible. Their brevity also makes them convenient shelters. The editor can exercise substantial power without exposing a comparison, and the author cannot tell whether the obstacle is a mathematical defect, a mismatch of scope, or the editor's unfamiliarity with the work.

Zeilberger's accounts of rejected computational papers are worth collecting for precisely this reason. Opinions 118 and 121 concern the same work with Andrew Sills on Rademacher's conjecture, with links to journal decisions. Opinion 153 concerns a different desk rejection and alleges hostility to explicit formulas and computer assistance. The linked material permits examination of particular decisions. The author's interpretation of motive remains an allegation. Counting two essays about the same rejection history as two independent cases would manufacture a pattern. [36] [37] [41]

His earlier complaint about Mathematical Reviews adds a related question about visibility. Which short papers and computer-associated contributions receive the ordinary machinery of notice? The editorial reply belongs beside the complaint. An omission is an event. Establishing why it occurred takes further evidence. The distinction matters because the professional consequences of being overlooked can be real even when a proposed explanation is wrong. [43]

Opinion 112 challenges the different treatment of computer-assisted work and erroneous conventional proofs. That challenge identifies a comparison worth making, while the complete editorial record remains necessary for a verdict on a submission. What burden of checking was imposed? Was it also imposed on comparable work using more familiar methods? Could a correctable defect be repaired, or did the form of the work itself become disqualifying? A standard deserves respect when its application can survive those questions. [35]

The defence of editorial discretion becomes shabby when it demands that authors trust a judgment whose substance the decision-maker refuses to articulate. Time is limited, and a rejection need not become an endless debate. A concise explanation can still name the missing contribution, the decisive comparison, or the unresolved objection. Prestige should make an editor better able to give that account. When it merely makes the editor less obliged to give one, the institution has converted esteem into exemption.

Gowers and the limits of revolt within the hierarchy

Gowers's defence of combinatorics is frequently invoked as a challenge to mathematical snobbery. Its scope needs closer attention. The Two Cultures of Mathematics explicitly concerns divisions within pure mathematics. More revealingly, his explanation on his papers page says that he accepts the theoreticians' criteria of worthwhile mathematics and seeks to show that combinatorics satisfies them. That is a defence of an undervalued subject under an existing standard. [97] [103]

Such a defence may expose a genuine misjudgment. It still leaves a question for everyone whose work fails to resemble the standard's preferred examples. Why should the worth of an algorithm, a model, an experiment, or a body of painstaking numerical work depend on its admission to a conception of depth developed elsewhere? A hierarchy can broaden its membership while keeping its power to determine the terms of membership.

The distinction is important because a successful appeal can become evidence that the system needs no further examination. Combinatorics has acquired distinguished representatives, therefore the criteria must have been capacious all along. The reasoning overlooks the labour required to make those representatives legible, the work that remains outside, and the possibility that the criteria recognize some achievements much more readily than others.

The same issue appears in Gowers's public defence of mathematics. His comparison gives the pure problem the attribute “interesting” and the potentially useful problem the attribute “uninteresting.” He also describes the temptation to prefer mathematical elegance to model accuracy. Bruss challenges the criterion directly from the perspective of probability and useful modelling. The apparent neutrality of mathematical interest is exactly what is in dispute. [104] [94]

There is nothing mysterious about a researcher preferring the questions that most engage them. Trouble begins when that preference passes through the word mathematics and emerges as an account of what society should value. A chosen internal audience becomes the imagined universal audience. A problem becomes interesting because that audience finds it interesting. Then the audience's judgment is cited to justify supporting the problem. The argument has changed its scale while concealing the change in its premises.

Steinhardt and Christiano press a related question about the inference from interconnectedness to the practical value of pure research. Their 2011 comments ask what the connections establish about comparative investment and causal dependence. Steinhardt subsequently withdraws a stronger claim about complete disconnection from reality. The remaining question concerns which work most effectively advances the ends being invoked in its defence. [95] [96]

A network of connections is a starting point for that inquiry. The relevant paths may run in different directions, carry very different amounts of useful information, and require very different investments. The existence of a route between two subjects leaves open how often anyone travels it, what they bring back, and whether another route serves the same purpose better. A diagram of mathematical unity cannot by itself allocate the next research grant.

There is also a quieter way for pure mathematics to become the default meaning of mathematics. Higham observes that the broadly titled Princeton Companion to Mathematics is essentially about pure mathematics. The editorial concentration may be deliberate and defensible. Its cultural effect is still worth naming. One part of the discipline receives the unqualified name, while another needs an adjective and a companion volume of its own. [98]

Gowers's review of Paul Nahin provides a more pointed linguistic example. In discussing a numerical treatment of a pursuit problem, he says that Nahin “abandons the mathematics.” The review also welcomes engineering insights and some simulations. The phrase nevertheless marks the numerical transition as an exit from mathematics. It is a small piece of language with a large presumption tucked inside it. [105]

These examples warrant criticism of Gowers's evaluative frame. They also clarify what that criticism should demand. A useful comparison between analytic and numerical treatments would explain what each establishes, which features each reveals, and what each costs. A profession accustomed to calling one part of that work mathematics and treating the other as an auxiliary service has already decided the cultural question before beginning the intellectual comparison.

Criticism has to name its charge

Zeilberger's disagreements with Gowers vary considerably. His review of the short introduction objects to the treatment of existing computer-assisted achievements and the positive connotations of mainstream status. Opinion 137 attacks a proof-centered conception of mathematics. Those are disputes about what mathematical work should recognize and reward. [99] [100]

The publishing criticism has a different object. Opinion 120 demands a broader boycott of subscription publishing. Opinion 156 questions the status allocation that survives in an overlay-journal model. A journal can abandon expensive distribution and retain the social authority conferred by selective endorsement. Removing the price barrier leaves open who gets to pronounce work worthy of notice. [101] [38]

Opinion 169, concerning the Hill affair, is more personal still. Zeilberger objects to combining condemnation of disputed publication procedures with criticism of the paper's merit. Gowers's post condemns suppression while arguing that the paper was weak, and it links subsequent statements from participants. The episode presents distinct questions about procedure, technical merit and public framing. They require distinct answers. [102]

The wider lesson concerns the use of criticism to distribute attention. A weak paper can be mishandled. A strong paper can receive an appropriately demanding review. A critic can correctly identify one defect while distracting from another. Anyone who occupies a prominent position should be able to keep these propositions in view at the same time. Expertise becomes especially unhelpful when it encourages its possessor to act as if one judgment settles every question in the room.

They are also harder on professional gatekeepers. A vague accusation of elitism is easy to wave away. A documented mismatch between a stated criterion and its application obliges a reply. Exact criticism deprives authority of its favourite refuge, the suggestion that the injured person simply failed to appreciate the standards.

What the credit system teaches

Thurston's account of mathematical progress asks what happens when recognition attaches chiefly to identifiable theorem production. His discussion of status, collaboration and the development of foliations describes consequences that can arise even without a conscious wish to obstruct anyone. A person can become conspicuously successful while the conditions for other people to enter the subject deteriorate. [18]

The uncomfortable possibility is that a profession may reward the visible termination of a problem while neglecting the work that makes problems approachable. Explanation, examples, notation, software, testing, translation between specialties and patient conversation can change what other people are able to do. Their contributions are often distributed across a community and difficult to assign to a single moment of triumph. A credit system organized around the triumph will systematically underdescribe the achievement.

That underdescription can become a style of personality. If the recognized unit of value is the theorem with one's name attached, another person's need for explanation begins to look like an interruption. Work on exposition seems like a concession to the less able. A careful implementation looks like something to delegate. The profession can then mistake a refusal to perform connective labour for evidence that a person belongs at a higher intellectual altitude.

Bass's reflections on teaching put pressure on this altitude. His 2025 interview describes mathematicians invoking position and theorem-based prestige when dealing with education, while showing contempt for teachers' knowledge. His earlier essay includes self-examination about communicating mathematics and the field's sense of intellectual aristocracy. [57] [58]

The governing question is what the achievement qualifies its possessor to know. A proof in one subject may demonstrate extraordinary mathematical power. Understanding how a particular group of students learns requires observation, experience and inquiry directed at those students. Treating the first accomplishment as an exemption from the second task is intellectual laziness fortified by reputation.

Teachers, numerical analysts and programmers often encounter this laziness in a characteristic form. Their practical constraints are regarded as a lower layer to which theory will eventually descend. Yet those constraints may contain the hardest part of the problem. A method that cannot be implemented, a theorem whose hypotheses cannot be verified, or an explanation that communicates with nobody outside the initiating circle has left consequential work for someone else. The remaining work does not become easy because a prestigious person stops looking at it.

IV. Making the criticism answerable

A dispute that exposes the method

The 2007 controversy around Koblitz's account of mathematics and cryptography offers an unusually inspectable argument over disciplinary authority. The original discusses publication habits, security claims and tensions between mathematical and cryptographic cultures. Katz, Goldreich, Krawczyk, Wigderson and others objected to its generalizations and characterization of their field. Their replies make it possible to examine particular charges and countercharges rather than inherit a verdict. [66] [67] [69] [70] [71]

Barak's reply isolates three questions that a sweeping judgment can run together. What exactly is claimed? How credible are the assumptions? Is the proof correct? His distinction is useful far beyond cryptography. A proof may correctly establish an implication whose hypotheses inadequately describe the intended situation. A model may be valuable while a published proof contains a repairable gap. Calling everything bad mathematics saves the critic the trouble of identifying where the difficulty lies. [68]

Trevisan attacks the passage from technical criticism to personal disparagement. Mitzenmacher examines the misleading contrast between one excellent paper and a stream of worthless ones, while accepting that publication culture can deserve criticism. Eppstein supplies a deliberately satirical response. Read together, they show how rapidly an argument over standards can become a struggle over which profession is entitled to regard itself as serious. [72] [73] [74]

Goldreich's methodological defence adds the positive task. Clear definitions and rigorous inference can expose threats that ordinary experience fails to anticipate. An account of applied mathematics that treats its demands for proof as a pretentious imitation of pure mathematics would miss the reasons those demands arise from the application itself. Practical work can require greater care precisely because a result has to survive contact with an adversary, a machine or the world. [75]

The cultural criticism is therefore exacting. Someone who dismisses an applied field must understand why its practitioners chose their standards, what failures those standards were designed to prevent, and how the proposed criticism improves them. A display of mathematical refinement supplies none of that work automatically. The person who knows how to criticize a proof still has to learn what the proof is trying to protect.

The critics have to read

The Avigad–Zeilberger exchange makes the obligation to read one's opponent particularly clear. Opinion 182 attacks Avigad as a defender of conceptual superiority. Avigad replies that he supports computers and intended to examine a common attitude, including the role computation can play within it. Larson's accompanying letter presses the importance of useful algorithms against an inward-looking conception of significance. The exchange contains disagreements worth pursuing, but the reply changes what can responsibly be attributed to Avigad. [40] [79] [80] [81]

This is a necessary discipline for an attack on snobbery. An anthology becomes intellectually cheap if it treats every description of a prejudice as an endorsement, or every defender of an unpopular method as a hero. That habit reproduces the defect under examination. The reader gets a cast of approved and disapproved people instead of an account of the work.

There is ample room for hard criticism after the distinction is made. One can examine how an author defines understanding, whether practical success receives adequate weight, and whether a proposed standard is applied consistently. These questions can expose a hierarchy even when the author explicitly supports the activities it subordinates. Declared sympathy does not settle the allocation of intellectual prestige.

Rigor and access

The Jaffe–Quinn controversy concerns both standards of proof and the organization of discovery. Their original proposal and subsequent reply argue for clearly identifying the status of mathematical claims. The reply also emphasizes the needs of people outside leading centres who depend on reliable written accounts. Access to informal expertise is distributed unequally, and prestige can conceal how much work that expertise is doing. [77] [78]

The responses press other costs. Atiyah objects to an authoritarian picture of discovery, Uhlenbeck questions the special standing assigned to elite theoretical activity, and Thom ridicules professional policing. Their disagreement shows that formal standards, credit and the right to define mathematical legitimacy can become entangled. [14] [15] [16]

A well-connected mathematician may learn which conjecture is dependable, where a proof is missing, and whom to ask for the crucial explanation. A distant reader sees a citation. If the literature relies on tacit knowledge possessed by a small circle, the profession's promise of open verification has acquired a social admission requirement. The insistence on a clear written record can then serve an egalitarian purpose.

Conversely, rigorous presentation can be used to erase the people whose ideas made the proof possible. A discovery appears fully formed, wrapped in a perfected language that conceals the questions, examples and failed attempts through which it became intelligible. Whoever supplies the last accepted form can then seem to have supplied the entire achievement. The cost of that illusion falls on both credit and understanding.

These failures share a structure. In each, an account of mathematical work omits a dependency and allocates authority as if the omission were harmless. Sometimes it omits a proof. Sometimes it omits the explanation needed to understand the proof. Sometimes it omits the computational, experimental or pedagogical work that made the claim worth proving. Exactness requires recovering the dependency that matters in the case at hand.

Authority that can answer

The documents converge on an ugly asymmetry. The high-status mathematician's preference can arrive as a verdict. The unfamiliar researcher must arrive with a proof of the right to be heard. An established school can treat its vocabulary as the natural language of intelligence. A rival must explain every word. A senior figure's impatience is accepted as discernment, while the recipient's irritation becomes evidence of immaturity. That is a petty arrangement, however impressive the mathematics produced by some of its beneficiaries.

Pure mathematics supplies exceptionally refined ways to evade this criticism. The critic has misunderstood depth. The example is too special. The calculation merely verifies. The implementation belongs to somebody else's job. Each answer may occasionally be correct. None earns correctness from the professional standing of the person delivering it. A community that understands the danger of assuming the conclusion should recognize the danger of a hierarchy that defines its own preferences as the evidence of its superiority.

Rota, Thurston, Arnold, Kahan, Trefethen and the other writers in this collection disagree about a great deal. They do not supply one doctrine to memorize. They supply occasions on which the habits of mathematical judgment become visible. Some show contempt stated openly. Others show a slight qualification doing the work of a demotion. Still others show the critic failing the very standard being demanded of an opponent. The point of keeping the replies is to make every claim answerable, including the claims in an attack on snobbery.

Refactor's own mathematical practice supplies a concrete test. A representation earns its place by exposing a constraint, enabling an operation and carrying a justified answer back to the original question. A proof earns attention through what it establishes and makes possible. An explanation succeeds when someone can use it. None of those achievements becomes smaller because it is accessible, computational, motivated by a physical problem, or expressed without the vocabulary of an admired school.

The corresponding standard of conduct is equally direct. If a result is trivial, show the reduction. If a method is inadequate, identify the failure. If a subject is unimportant, state the comparison and defend it. If someone asks for an example, either give one or explain the obstruction. If an earlier judgment was wrong, correct the record where the judgment did its damage. These obligations ask for the ordinary work of responsible evaluation. Resisting them while demanding respect is exactly the pettiness under examination.

Pure mathematicians do not earn a monopoly on intellectual seriousness by choosing questions whose standards they can control. Their finest work can survive contact with other forms of inquiry. Their worst professional habits depend on avoiding it. The sneer is easiest when the speaker never has to make anything clear. Making the claim explicit, the comparison concrete and the judgment revisable is how that cheap authority loses its protection.
