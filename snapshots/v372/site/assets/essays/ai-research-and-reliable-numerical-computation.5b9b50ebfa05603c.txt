Abstraction is no refuge from AI
What did you throw away?

Put dye in every other cell of a ring divided into four equal parts. Shift the measuring grid by half a cell. Every new cell covers equal parts dyed and clear fluid. The four averages tell you exactly how much dye there is. They give you no way to tell which pair you dyed. My first bet is on incidence geometry inside numerical PDE. To decide what to save before the old mesh disappears, I would take an experienced numerical analyst and an agent over two agents. From combinatorics I would take two practices with me. Doron Zeilberger chooses what a machine should guess so that a few identities can prove a count at every size. Richard Brualdi asks what the row and column totals force in every matrix that has them.

1. What a breakthrough leaves open

If you are moving deeper into abstraction to outrun AI, you are too late. In representation theory, algebraic geometry, abstract algebra, algebraic combinatorics and theoretical PDE, machines are already doing the conceptual work that was supposed to keep them out. Machines have found a missing dimension bound in a Lie algebra, connected arithmetic eigenweights to symmetric-group representations and improved a bound in an inverse PDE problem. In algebraic combinatorics, the authors of qtBench judged ten of OpenAI’s proposed solutions genuine. One gives a bijection explaining why exchanging two dimensions leaves a family of q,t-Narayana polynomials unchanged. [1, 3, 8, 16, 99, 162]

Four vertices are enough to expose the difficulty. Take a cycle with alternating strong and weak edges. A solver groups the vertices joined by each strong edge so it can correct errors shared by the pair. Now exchange the weights while keeping the old pairs. The matrix has the same size, the same pattern of nonzeros, the same diagonal, even the same eigenvalues. Give both vertices of one new strong pair error +1 and both vertices of the other error −1. The residual sums to zero on each old pair. From those two sums alone, the coarse solver sees exactly what it would see if the answer were already right.

Those two sums have lost the relation between the strong edges and the old pairs. In OpenAI’s Deligne-Drinfeld proof a projection throws away a whole variable, yet the defining relations recover what the theorem needs. The Lie algebra and the graph pose one question. Which distinction must the next calculation preserve? On the graph we need a different observation. In the algebra the apparent loss is precisely what makes the proof possible. A machine found the missing bound. Mathematicians then asked what the projection meant. [1, 2]

The second discovery can be bigger than a repair. OpenAI’s model used number fields of growing degree to put unexpectedly many pairs of points a unit distance apart in the plane. Four mathematicians saw a reason to revisit a different conjecture about adding and multiplying real numbers. Their resulting counterexample owes its main ideas to the four authors. They credit GPT-5.5 Pro with suggesting an elementary lemma that simplified the proof. A machine had changed what was worth trying. People saw where else it might work. [132, 133]

Machines have made that next move themselves. After42’s agents tried to understand how edge costs change the geometry of a random spanning tree. On a cycle a tree omits one edge. After averaging over independent, identically distributed costs, each edge is equally likely to be missing at every temperature. The calculation exposed what the one-tree comparison could miss. The agents chose a better question. Give two trees the same costs and sample each separately. How much can the distance between the same two vertices differ? A person had specified a broad research goal. The decision to compare two trees was the agents’ own. The general conjecture remains open. [134]

I expect a specialist to add more by deciding what a solver must remember than by making it faster on a score everybody accepts. A failed test gives both teams a reason to investigate. A passing test can conceal the very loss that matters to the next user. The numerical analyst who has needed a local concentration and received only a conserved total has a reason to look there anyway. The geometer’s move is to exhibit two fields that agree on every stored reading and force different answers to the user’s question. Now we have a theorem about what the next solver must keep. Zeilberger works backward from a different demand. Given a formula conjectured to hold at every size, what could the machine guess that would let us prove it? On the mesh the future question can still be unknown when the data are saved. In the ansatz the conclusion can already be written down while the object that will certify it still has to be invented. My sumset papers give a machine rival that same habit in another setting. In Kasparov’s freestyle chess both sides knew the object of the game. Here either team can discover what is worth playing for. [101, 105, 123, 130, 134, 145, 147, 151, 163] [What Sustains Mathematical Expertise](https://refactor.press/essays/what-sustains-mathematical-expertise#sustaining-independent-capability)


2. What the OpenAI breakthroughs actually show

A proof can turn on deciding what to forget. The Deligne-Drinfeld conjecture identifies the rational Grothendieck-Teichmüller Lie algebra with the free graded Lie algebra on one generator in each odd degree starting at three. A rational lower bound was known. Thomas Willwacher credits OpenAI with the missing upper bound and gives a shorter, AI-assisted proof. The equations have integer coefficients. Passing to characteristic two can enlarge their solution space, so an upper bound there also bounds its rational dimension. [1]

In characteristic two write A = x², B = y and C = [x,y]. Now set A to zero. At weight three the projection does this. [1]

P₃ = [A,B] + [B,C],    P₃(0,B,C) = [B,C].

The first term disappears. A representation of the four-strand braid Lie algebra turns the defining equations into a formula recovering the whole polynomial from its projection. On the constrained space the projection is injective. Its image lies in Lie(C,[B,C]), small enough to count in weights at least three. The upper bound was the missing half of the conjecture. OpenAI also gave an independent argument for injectivity of the original map from the free graded Lie algebra, for which Brown’s earlier work already supplied the rational lower bound. [1]

A correct proof left another piece of mathematics to invent. Leila Schneps and Benjamin Enriquez reworked the argument. Willwacher credits them with inspiring a simpler formula for the four-strand representation, and credits AI tools with helping him understand and restructure the proof. Nikita Markarian identified the projection with the leading part of a geometric operation. Take the double cover that sends z to z² and fill a puncture. He constructed an inverse on the image and recovered the algebraic reconstruction in leading terms. That interpretation is a new result to use. It does not retroactively give the interpreters the original upper bound. [1, 2]

In the unit-distance problem the machine’s choice of high-degree fields changed another research agenda. Nine mathematicians verified, simplified and generalized the construction. Bloom, Sawin, Schildkraut and Zhelezov then used large totally real number fields for a different purpose. Their first attempt carried over the unit-distance construction’s requirement for small primes that split in the field. They discovered that units sufficed. A short geometric progression has few products but is too sparse to control sums. They replaced it with a denser collection of units and replaced an interval with a box of algebraic integers. For arbitrarily large finite sets A of real numbers, both the sumset and the product set are bounded by the size of A raised to a fixed power strictly below two. The authors used GPT early as a sounding board. Its lemma simplified one step. The idea of carrying this construction from geometry to arithmetic, and the main ideas that made it work, were theirs. [132, 133] Yichen Huang later gave an April model snapshot the precise real sum-product statement and told it the statement was true. With search and tools disabled, seven of eight runs produced proofs Huang judged correct. Some avoided the use of units central to the human construction. The four authors had recognized a target worth reopening. Once the target was named, machines could do much of the mathematics that made reopening it worthwhile. [152]

The traffic can run the other way. On a problem about arithmetic eigenweights, Tony Feng credits Google DeepMind’s Aletheia with making the connection to symmetric-group representation theory and determining the weights for all classical groups. He describes his mathematical role after building the agent as rewriting its work into a paper. In Das and Fox’s proof of the Murty-Simon conjecture, OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol supplied the edge bound and the even-order equality case. The authors simplified the argument and established which graphs attain equality at every order. A person can edit a proof, extend it, explain it or launch another investigation from it. Those are different contributions. [3, 11]

Blind review offers a different check. First Proof gave each system twenty-four hours to tackle ten problems without human help. GPT-6 Astra earned credit on seven and the independently developed ProofCouncil on nine. On the Darcy inverse problem all three reviewers judged ProofCouncil’s mathematics essentially correct. It removed a logarithmic loss in the human upper bound and earned full marks for correctness and exposition, zero for attribution. In TheoPhysEval, some apparent Astra errors sent the experts back to their own reference solutions. They found mistakes there and corrected them. The Darcy reviewers wanted Richter’s inversion formula credited where the proof used it. Contributing a better bound and explaining where its ingredients came from are two jobs, and the reviewers could tell which one the system had done. [8, 9]

OpenAI’s October catalogue contains 721 manuscripts in 373 families, selected from work on roughly 4,000 posed problems. Its subject table has a PDE category and no numerical-analysis category. It gives no attempted-problem totals by field. Even a long chain of proofs leaves a question about who chose its destination. Part II of the arithmetic-progression trace opens with a human prompt naming the quasipolynomial strengthening. The model found where precision was lost and devised the proof move. The release establishes mathematical reach. Its counts give us no success rate for autonomous sparse PDE research. [4, 5, 6, 7, 16] [Appendix A](#mathematical-and-benchmark-record)

A direct numerical test cuts against any easy inference from that silence. Johannes Brust and Tamara Kolda asked how to solve a tensor-fitting system from scattered observations using preconditioned conjugate gradients. Five model answers, including OpenAI’s, found the same saving in the matrix-vector product. They evaluated only the entries the observations required. The authors incorporated the method and report a further two- to fivefold speedup over their already accelerated solver. They traced the idea to earlier work on sparse kernel learning that none of the answers cited. The researchers supplied the problem. The models found a better way to do the work. [155]


3. How the partners change each other’s mathematics

Christian Coester wanted to know how much farther an online algorithm must travel than a competitor who knows the coming requests. Each request is a set the algorithm has to reach. An earlier lower bound in the plane used three stages of line requests and six constants. Coester wanted a continuously moving line. GPT-5 initially reproduced the old bound. Coester says a fleeting suggestion of π/2 in its reasoning may have helped him see the semicircle traced as the line rotates. Following the semicircle would cost the online algorithm π/2 while the optimal route cost one. To force the bound even on an algorithm that left the arc, his construction needed to switch pivots. That introduced a defect. It could switch pivots infinitely often at one instant and never move on. [130]

GPT-5 supplied the repair. Require a small positive separation before switching pivots, then let that separation tend to zero. For the higher-dimensional problem Coester was trying to make the construction work in three dimensions. The model suggested applying the planar argument separately in orthogonal two-dimensional planes. Coester corrected errors in the execution. His geometry gave the machine a viable problem to solve. Its decomposition gave him a proof route his focus on three dimensions had obscured. There is no record of what a second, equally equipped machine strategist would have achieved. There is a precise record of how each partner changed the other’s work. [130]

In the Grothendieck-constant project the decisive human act was to stop a search that was still producing mathematics. Six variations on a rounding scheme had failed. The operators asked for a theorem limiting the entire class. The possibility of reversing the search was already in the brief. The agent found the Naor-Regev result that could turn a sufficiently general ceiling on the schemes into a lower bound on the constant. Fourteen later sessions proved restricted sublemmas while the bound for the whole class stayed out of reach. The operators redirected the effort again. [105]

This time the agent found the reason the earlier arguments would not extend. The quantities they controlled did not average properly when schemes were mixed. Two Hermite coefficients did. An affine inequality in those coefficients survives taking mixtures. The operators pushed for a member of the new family that could actually be proved. One complete result was the lower bound 6π/11. The human recognized that another local theorem would leave the main question open. The machine invented the mathematical relation that survived the passage to the full class. [105] On Erdős’s minimum-overlap problem the investigators gave the Station agents the opposite brief. Improve the upper bound on how much two complementary parts of an interval must overlap under translation. The agents coupled two Fourier measurements of that overlap and proved a lower bound above 0.380552. They chose to turn the inequality around and delivered a certificate for the direction they chose. [106]

A short comparison shows what directing a search can buy. AlphaEvolve was asked to build a long ±1 sequence whose sums along every progression d, 2d, 3d, … stay between −2 and 2. It reached length 200. With a hint to try approximately multiplicative sequences it found length 380 in the same amount of search time. The known optimum is 1,160. The hint changed where the machine looked on a problem with a known answer. Its authors did not compare the time to devise that hint with the time a machine adviser would have needed. [131]

Archon measures the effect of advice at a proof checkpoint. Its planning and Lean agents had three obligations left in a proof of unique factorization. A supervisor supplied a Krull-domain blueprint. The system kept its own Kaplansky strategy, borrowed intermediate observations and finished in 2 hours 12 minutes. Without the blueprint the same system finished in 3 hours 43 minutes. That is a time saving on one continuation of one proof. It leaves out the time spent writing the blueprint and gives no other investigator a turn at writing one. A human sped up a machine team. Who else could have done so remains open. [90]

The reason to bring a specialist is strongest when their independent experience changes a choice with consequences. The Grothendieck operators knew what fourteen sessions of restricted lemmas had failed to settle. Bolzano gives the reverse handoff. Its agents invented a nested search in which each collision determines the next place to look. Pavel Hubáček found the obstruction showing why revealing a collision could still leave a reduction empty-handed. An equally persistent machine rival deserves its own failures, notebooks and time to change its mind. Comparing the partners on the theorem each eventually produces is harder than comparing their advice. It is also closer to what a mathematician wants to know. [105, 125, 128]

There is already a comparison of two machines starting from the same paper. Both tried extending a result about losing positions in Wythoff Nim, a game played with two piles of tokens. The predicted integer shift failed in the larger family. One agent retreated to the original game. The other followed two citations to a theorem with a fractional shift and proposed a conjecture covering both cases. Across a blinded review of twelve pairs, an author of the seed papers preferred eight conjectures from the method that tracked changes between theorems and four from the ordinary search. All were proposals. Two machine investigators had found different mathematics to pursue. [135]

Does adding a person improve that choice? In a blinded language-research study, one researcher’s reranking replaced 32 of the machine’s 49 choices. Both lists scored above human-written proposals on novelty. Difference of taste is easy to establish. Its payoff takes longer. In a follow-up, 43 researchers executed assigned projects. The machine-origin ideas lost significantly more of their initial ratings on all four shared measures. Across the 43 projects the final differences between origins were statistically inconclusive. A plausible role for a specialist is to recognize which attractive proposal will survive contact with the work. [136, 137]

TasteVal put a human or a model in charge of the same coding agent. Each chose the next experiment after seeing what the last one bought. The strongest model declined one of the eight machine-learning tasks. Across the other seven it had an estimated 2.25-fold advantage in experimental compute efficiency against the best human attempt per task. The 95 percent confidence interval ran from 1.16 to 4.29. Here two machines could beat an expert directing the same instrument. The investigators had supplied the score. Choosing an experiment that raises a score and deciding which score deserves to govern the work are different achievements. [159]


4. Zeilberger’s ansatz and Brualdi’s matrix questions

George Andrews and David Robbins had conjectured a formula for the weighted count of totally symmetric plane partitions. Okada had reduced the count to a determinant. Koutschan, Kauers and Zeilberger asked what a machine could guess that would prove the formula. They chose the normalized cofactors of the last row. Each cofactor is the signed determinant left after deleting that row and one column. Divide each by the determinant at the preceding size. Nothing guaranteed that the resulting numbers would satisfy recurrences of a kind the computer could discover. Choosing to look for those recurrences was the ansatz. [163]

The choice came with an exact test. The last entry of the candidate vector must be one. Its dot product with every earlier row must vanish. Its dot product with the last row must give the ratio of two successive values of the conjectured formula. Small determinants supplied enough data to guess recurrences for the vector. The team proved the three identities, and induction gave the count at every size. Put that proof beside qtBench. A machine found a bijection for an algebraic-combinatorics problem. Zeilberger’s experimental school proved its counting formula with algebra. Both crossed the supposed boundary. What I would learn from Zeilberger is how to choose the object that does the proving. [162, 163]

Wilf and Zeilberger had shown how a suitable hypergeometric sum could be proved by finding an identity that telescopes. Check the boundary terms and an initial value, and one certificate settles the sum for every admissible index. Zeilberger’s later program WILF searched for the counting scheme itself. Give it permutations that avoid the patterns 123 and 132, and allow it to inspect prefixes of length two. The count it yields is easy to see once you have the rule. The next entry must be one of the two largest remaining. Two later entries larger than it would make a forbidden triple whichever order they came in. For a permutation of length n there are two choices at each of the first n − 1 positions and one at the last. That counts the permutations at every size. [82, 117]

Zeilberger built the empirical program HERB before the rigorous WILF. Experiments on small cases helped him design the search that could certify a recurrence. When WILF exhausted its depth allowance, it returned zero. That meant the search had found no scheme within its rules. A deeper or differently organized search could still pay. A structural experiment can be cheap, have an honest stopping point and pay in exact answers at every size. In Theorems for a Price Zeilberger also asked what one would pay for a proof after an experiment had already supplied strong evidence. HERB helped its author design WILF. WILF then found counting schemes for itself. Every later user inherited a piece of Zeilberger’s mathematical judgment. To see what a live collaborator still contributes, give the agent the program and change the problem. [117, 121]

John Napier knew what he wanted logarithms to save. In his 1614 preface he described the time lost multiplying large numbers and the errors the work invited. He offered different numbers on which simpler operations would do the job. Michael Atiyah took that familiar motive much further in 1984. Because computers excel at long calculations, he wrote, “they will not search for alternatives.” He had already observed in the same essay that computer time makes faster algorithms valuable. The historical motive is real. The prediction failed. A machine need not find arithmetic tedious to discover a way around it. [112, 81]

Once a research move can be written down, it can travel without its author. Shanmu Jin adapted the procedure made public for the cycle-double-cover problem to ask for a proof of Crouzeix’s conjecture. Different machine branches attacked one another’s proposals. The decisive theorem emerged during a run of roughly sixteen hours. Townsend, Greenbaum and Crouzeix examined the proof and reported that they believe it correct. Jin chose the target and organized the search. The model found the theorem. Any team that inherits the procedure begins with more than its own ingenuity. [12, 17]

Zeilberger’s ambition already has descendants who set problems for people. Asked about graph invariants, TxGraffiti conjectured that in a connected cubic graph the independence number is at most three halves of the total domination number. Caro, Davila, Henning and Pepper proved the inequality for every regular degree at least two with the factor changed to half the degree, and characterized equality. FunSearch produced a constructor with independent cyclic symmetries. Its researchers recognized them and restricted the next search to symmetric admissible sets. One system gave people a theorem to generalize. The other gave them a representation in which to pose a new search. Neither project gave a second machine the same opening. [83, 117, 138]

In [Continuity as Organized Discrete Data](https://refactor.press/essays/continuity-as-organized-discrete-data#choosing-how-far-to-look), every row of a table starts with ones and ends in zeros. Row m has exactly m ones. Every row represents zero in the limit. Pick its mth entry and the diagonal is all ones. The limit was the right object for identifying the value of each row. To use the rows together we need to know how far along each one to look. That move is worth learning. Ask what the old identification licensed, exhibit the next operation on which it fails, and recover just enough information to perform that operation. Sometimes the result is a schedule for a calculation. Sometimes it is a new mathematical object.

What the margins of a matrix force

Richard Brualdi and Jeffrey Ross ask how much of a zero-one matrix can be recovered after only its row and column totals have been saved. Put four ones down the diagonal of a four-by-four matrix. Every row and every column has total one. The upper-left two-by-two block contains two ones. Exchange the first two columns with the last two. The same eight totals now describe a matrix with no ones in that block. Those totals are the degrees of a bipartite graph. The block count asks which rows actually meet which columns. [164]

Brualdi and Ross proved something much stronger than the example. Take all binary matrices with the specified margins, assuming at least one exists. If every entry can vary somewhere in that family, then every block formed by a nonempty proper subset of the rows and a nonempty proper subset of the columns can vary in its count of ones. Turn it around. If even one such block has a fixed total, the margins must have forced at least one individual entry somewhere in the matrix. This is the matrix theory I would take to a numerical PDE problem. Given what the solver records, which later questions have the same answer in every field compatible with that record? [164]

The machine is already a rival on Brualdi’s ground. OpenAI constructed a fixed 66-by-66 zero-one pattern and large matrices in which a uniform sample of rows and columns is a poor guide to how much work it takes to remove the pattern. A copy must preserve the two orders and match every zero and one. Along the constructed family, the copies become rarer than any power of the fraction of entries that must be changed to remove them all. An investigator who trusts the sampled counts can overlook an expensive defect. A machine built the matrices that expose it. I would pursue this kind of combinatorics because a good construction tells us exactly where a test fails. [165]

In my own [Taiko product structures](https://refactor.press/papers/taiko-girth-obstruction), translating a group-ring construction into incidence geometry exposed intersection constraints that the coarse counts concealed. The same habit has a use on a mesh. Before counting, ask what can meet what. Before coarsening, ask which distinctions can survive it. The four-cycle lets us follow one such choice all the way to the calculation it breaks. [19, 20, 44, 80]


5. What a sparse graph can afford to forget

Start with a finite weighted graph. Orient its edges, let B be the edge-vertex incidence matrix with -1 at each edge's tail and +1 at its head, and put its positive edge weights in the diagonal matrix W. The graph Laplacian L has the following quadratic form.

L = B^T W B,     x^T L x = sum_{edges {i,j}} w_ij (x_i - x_j)^2.

For a connected graph, the constant vectors form the kernel. Ground a vertex or work on the mean-zero subspace to obtain a positive-definite problem. We will also use a positive diagonal shift. In either case we know exactly which quantity a change of representation must respect. [19, 26]

Elimination makes the central difficulty visible. Suppose a vertex is joined to three neighbors by edges of weight one. For fixed neighbor values, minimize the energy over the central value. The minimizer is their average. Substitution leaves exactly the energy of a triangle on the three neighbors, each new edge having weight one third. For a star with positive incident-weight vector a, elimination gives the Schur complement below.

S = diag(a) - a a^T / s,     s = sum_i a_i;     a = (1,1,1)^T gives triangle weights 1/3.

Figure: Eliminating the center of a three-edge unit star produces a triangle with edge weights 1/3. For any fixed values at the three remaining vertices, the triangle has exactly the star's minimum energy over the eliminated central value. Both retain the constant-vector nullspace.

Elimination preserves minimized energy exactly. Repeated elimination also couples previously unconnected variables, creating fill. Retaining every new interaction can destroy the economy of a sparse input. Discarding interactions changes the energy that made the elimination correct. Spectral sparsification seeks a much smaller graph whose energy approximates the original for every vector. Effective-resistance sampling assigns importance using the surrounding network as well as the edge weight. [19]

Batson, Spielman and Srivastava show that a reweighted subgraph with at most ceil((n − 1)/ε²) edges can preserve every vector’s energy between factors (1 − ε)² and (1 + ε)² for 0 < ε < 1. This is what justifies forgetting edges. The four-cycle uses another kind of compression. Relaxation removes the errors it can shrink cheaply and leaves the rest to a coarse space. Which errors belong there depends on the actual operator. [20, 84]

Accuracy has a similar trap. In the following exact two-variable system, the second variable is weakly weighted.

A = diag(1, 10^-8),  b = (1, 10^-8)^T,  x = (1,1)^T,  y = (1,0)^T.

The relative residual in the infinity norm is 10^-8, while the relative forward error in the same norm is 1. The proposed answer loses the entire second component. Interpreting a residual therefore requires conditioning information and a specified error measure. LAPACK's error analysis makes that distinction explicit. [21]

A more discriminating inexpensive observation exposes this particular failure. With r = b - A y and absolute values taken entrywise, the componentwise backward error is

omega_c = max_i |r_i| / (|A| |y| + |b|)_i = 1.

Its denominator is (2, 10^-8), so the lost second component remains visible. Computing this quantity needs a sparse matrix-vector product, without an inverse. Forward-error control still requires the appropriate conditioning information. The example shows why choosing the right observable can be more useful than refining an aggregate residual. [21]

Even a perfect componentwise check certifies only the equations put into the machine. Where did those equations come from? In scalar elliptic finite elements, Boman, Hendrickson and Vavasis join the nodes of each element in a weighted star. They prove that its assembled graph energy is comparable to the stiffness energy. This lets a graph preconditioner accelerate the original finite-element solve. The bound depends on element shape, quadrature and conductivity variation within an element. A large jump along element boundaries can be harmless to the comparison. Move it across an element and it enters the bound. To say how close the computed field is to the continuum solution still takes an approximation estimate. Each passage earns a different conclusion. [21, 52, 156]


6. When a change of geometry defeats the solver

The coarse solver on our four-cycle can do its assigned job exactly and leave the important error untouched. We can follow the failure from the choice of pairs to a repair, without guessing at the intentions of its designer. For a positive-definite matrix A and a full-column-rank matrix P of coarse directions, the exact correction is

A_c = P^T A P,     x_new = x + P A_c^(-1) P^T (b - A x).

For the error e = x_exact − x, the exact coarse correction removes every vector in the column space of P and leaves every vector with P^T A v = 0 untouched. Its error-propagation operator is

e_new = E_c e,     E_c = I - P (P^T A P)^(-1) P^T A.

Return to the four-cycle 1-2-3-4-1. Let A = L + ε I, with 0 < ε < 1. Give edges 12 and 34 weight one and the other edges weight ε. The two columns of P indicate the pairs {1,2} and {3,4}. Exchange the strong and weak edges to obtain A′. A relabeling takes A to A′, so the spectrum, diagonal and sparsity pattern all agree. The diagram follows the same error v = (1,−1,−1,1)^T through that exchange.

Figure: The shaded regions retain the original aggregates {1,2} and {3,4}. Thick edges have weight 1 and thin edges weight epsilon. After the weights are exchanged, the missed vector has constant sign on the new strong pairs {1,4} and {2,3}, but opposite signs within each retained aggregate. The diagonal shift epsilon I is present in both operators.

A' v = 3 epsilon v,     P^T A' v = 0.

The old pairs see zero coarse residual on both graphs. What changes is the work the coarse level must do. Before the exchange, Av = (2 + ε)v. Afterward, A′v = 3εv. The common diagonal is (1 + 2ε)I, so a weighted Jacobi step on a mode of eigenvalue λ multiplies it by 1 − ωλ/(1 + 2ε). With ω = 2/3 and small ε, the original multiplier approaches −1/3 and the new one approaches 1. An error that relaxation used to shrink now survives almost intact.

Grouping {1,4} and {2,3} puts v in the new coarse space. One exact coarse correction then removes it. On an unfamiliar graph we need an affordable way of discovering [which distinction the retained description misses](https://refactor.press/essays/when-a-description-becomes-a-verdict#enough-for-which-question). Adaptive smoothed aggregation and compatible relaxation already generate or test coarse directions. Bao, Cui and Li learn a coarse space from the errors the smoother leaves behind. Their sharper measure compares spaces regardless of which basis represents them. The Gram matrix for that comparison is small. Building it takes solves with the original matrix, so they use it offline. Can we get enough of the same information while a four-solve job is still worth saving? [20, 78, 79, 86]

A changed workload can reward a different decision for a second reason. In the CAST elimination study, a setting that won on the third Chimera graph with four solves per factor lost with 250. On the other four graphs the more expensive setting was still slower at 250. A solver needs to know which change mattered. Sampling more edges can improve a local error estimate and increase the fill at later eliminations. [48] [Appendix B](#solver-evidence-and-extensions) works through the timing and an exact triangle on which the mean graph conceals the error in every sample.

Which promises survive a change?

Some changes admit an inexpensive certificate. Let A = L(w) + μ I and A' = L(w') + μ I on the same graph, with positive edge weights and fixed μ > 0. Choose 0 < c <= 1 <= C so that every edge's new weight divided by its old weight lies between c and C. Comparing the edge energies and the common grounding term gives the first inequality below. For a fixed symmetric positive-definite preconditioner M with an already proved comparison alpha M <= A <= beta M, transitivity gives the second.

c A <= A' <= C A,     c alpha M <= A' <= C beta M.

One exact-arithmetic pass through the weight ratios gives a condition-number bound Cβ/(cα) for the fixed M on the new system. A small group of extreme changes can make those global constants pessimistic. Updating level matrices or smoothers changes M and requires fresh analysis. A floating-point certificate also needs rounding control. [Appendix C](#experimental-protocol) compares keeping the old hierarchy intact with updating its matrices or rebuilding it. [19, 52]

Four triangles and two straight lines

Incompressible Stokes flow gives a sharper reason to care about geometry. Draw four triangles around an interior vertex, with the four edges through it lying on two straight lines. Number the triangles in cyclic order. For a continuous piecewise-polynomial velocity, let q₁ through q₄ be its four one-sided divergence values at the vertex. Then q₁ − q₂ + q₃ − q₄ = 0. Continuity along an edge makes the two tangential derivatives there agree. On two straight lines those agreements cancel in the alternating sum. A pressure space meant to equal the range of the discrete divergence has to remember that relation. The abstract mesh graph cannot tell whether the two pairs of edges are straight. [140, 141]

Here is an exact check. Put the four outer vertices one unit east, north, west and south of the origin. Give the east vertex unit upward velocity and the other vertices, including the center, zero velocity. Interpolate linearly on each triangle. All four divergences vanish. Move only the center one tenth of a unit east, keeping the nodal velocities. Starting with the northeast triangle and going counterclockwise, the divergences are now 1/9, 0, 0 and −1/9. Their alternating sum is 2/9. An incidence relation has disappeared without a single edge changing its endpoints. On nearly aligned meshes the pressure mode that exact alignment would exclude can be poorly controlled. Gräßle, Bohne and Sauter impose the relation at nearly singular vertices as well. For their planar elements of velocity degree at least four, a fixed positive threshold gives a stability bound robust to near-alignment. The price is that the discrete velocity can have nonzero divergence. They bound that defect. Now the next problem has teeth. On a moving mesh with a specified mass-conservation tolerance, when should one change the pressure space, change the mesh itself, or replace the velocity approximation? A linear solver can drive its residual to zero while leaving that choice entirely unsettled. [140, 141]

The eddy-current equations need a different incidence relation. Interpolate a nodal field and take its discrete gradient, or take the gradient first and interpolate the edge field. The two paths must agree. Tuminaro and Glusa exhibit a coarse stencil whose allowed edges are disconnected. No choice of interpolation weights on those edges can make the paths agree. Adding edges repairs the obstruction. Their algorithm already does this. A fresh question is whether a transfer that satisfies the identity can also retain the low-energy modes cheaply as material coefficients change. [25, 43, 54, 78, 84]

A finer mesh can give stronger evidence for a false conclusion. In Arnold, Falk and Winther’s square-domain Maxwell example, continuous piecewise-linear vector fields on a crisscross mesh give seven positive eigenvalues converging to the right values. The eighth converges to a spurious value. Their alternative uses degrees of freedom adapted to the differential operators. For the mixed source problem, a discrete subcomplex and uniformly bounded projections that commute with differentiation give stability. Approximation and further spectral convergence hypotheses let the discrete calculation answer the continuum eigenvalue question. A correct incidence identity starts that argument. The estimates carry it back. [22]

I would look where those two passages come apart. On the Stokes patch the abstract triangles keep their neighbors while a straight-line relation disappears. In the eddy-current calculation, one missing connection makes the desired identity impossible. These are geometric facts with different consequences. Point-curve incidences in the work of Raz, Sharir and de Zeeuw, and wave-packet overlaps in Guth, Wang and Zhang, already carry counting arguments back to algebra and theoretical PDE. The numerical opportunity is to make intersections answer a question about a computed field at the accuracy a user needs. That kind of work has earned recognition in both numerical and theoretical mathematics. [22, 43, 140, 141, 142, 144, 149, 150]


7. Who recognizes the need for a different calculation?

Once the missing connection is named, either team can look for it. Who notices when a calculation needs a different observation in the first place? Nguyen’s cosmology study records two distinct occasions to change a calculation. In the first, the errors were already visible. Across 33 sessions a coding agent tuned an analytic projection while errors remained 8-86 percent. It had found an alternative path in the code and defended the path it was using. The physicist identified the missing directional dependence of baryon-acoustic-oscillation damping and proposed angular quadrature for the projection. The agent implemented it in one session. Four of six affected outputs came within one percent of their references. [101]

The second occasion was harder to see. A fitted scalar brought the remaining discrepancies within the tests. The physicist asked which term belonged inside the integral. Restoring its angular dependence removed the fitted parameter. In the first episode expertise redirected a visibly failing search. In the second it explained why an apparent success still needed a different calculation. Parts of this account were reconstructed from memory. The authors did not replay either checkpoint with a fresh autonomous team. [101]

In a spring simulation, multiplying the stiffness by 32 barely changed the deflection. All six runs hit the travel stop. The agent proposed changing the number of springs. The senior researcher asked how the load was shared and whether the stop had hidden the effect of stiffness. Give five models the project record and both proposed plans in either order, and all ten responses pick the senior researcher’s plan. Across 248 decisions selected from earlier human corrections, fresh researchers with AI available recognized the recorded judgment 73.4 percent of the time. The best tested model scored 63.5 percent. That is evidence for a human edge in recognizing a supplied alternative. In the spring case itself, inventing the alternative was the contribution. Once it was on the page, every tested model could recognize it. [61]

On a sign-uncertainty problem the agents found the turn themselves. Their evaluator accepted Laguerre polynomials with at most twenty prescribed positive double roots. An exact obstruction on 41 tail points showed that no construction in that family could bring the target below 0.315305. The agents retained the Laguerre basis, abandoned the prescribed roots and certified 0.3089 with a polynomial the evaluator could not score. The machines had turned failure into a theorem about the search itself. They found exactly which assumption to abandon. Anyone assigning that act to the human by definition has already lost the argument. [106]

A machine can also invent the missing experiment. In a virtual lab the brief asked for a material’s conductivity at room temperature. Strong heating made the material more conductive, an effect the simulator omitted. One agent pooled its hot runs and missed the answer. Astra tried gentle heating and recovered the room-temperature value. Nobody handed it the gentler experiment. The brief did hand it the temperature that mattered. [160]

Who decides which quantity matters? Isaacson and colleagues took the same electrode readings from a tank containing two agar lungs and an agar heart. The D-bar reconstruction separated the lungs and recovered about 93 percent of the known heart-to-lung conductivity range. The NOSER reconstruction recovered about 27 percent, yet the authors judged the lungs’ overall size and position more faithful in its image. An investigator who asks whether the lungs can be distinguished and one who asks how large they are have reasons to trust different features of the very same measurements. A numerical guarantee has to say which question it answers. [161]


8. What makes a new test decisive

The four-cycle showed an error that vanished from the coarse residual. We can test the answer against the original system. For symmetric positive-definite A and M, suppose we have proved αM ≤ A with α > 0. Given an approximation y to Ax = b, compute its residual r = b − Ay. Reversing the matrix inequality under inversion gives

||x - y||_A^2 = r^T A^(-1) r <= (1/alpha) r^T M^(-1) r.

This bounds the energy error. When solving with M is cheap and α is large enough to give a useful bound, the certificate is worth computing. After a weight change, use cα from Section 6 and recompute r against A′. For singular Laplacians, restrict the comparison to the orthogonal complement of the common kernel. An estimated spectral comparison, an approximate solve with M, and rounding in the residual and quadratic form each require control before the calculated number can certify the answer. [21, 52]

The shifted-Laplacian pilot also admits a conservative check independent of the preconditioner. Since A' >= μ I, any validated upper bound ρ on the Euclidean norm of r = b - A' y gives

||x - y||_A' <= ||r||_2 / sqrt(mu) <= rho / sqrt(mu).

For the shifted graph, a validated norm bound ρ thus certifies the absolute energy tolerance τ whenever ρ/√μ ≤ τ. This check works even when the quality of the updated hierarchy is unknown. It tells us whether the answer is good enough. Its conservatism can determine how long we keep iterating. [21, 52]

For a nonzero residual, call the ratio of the exact-residual bound to the true energy error R. On an eigenmode with eigenvalue λ, R = √(λ/μ). For the four-cycle’s missed mode this is exactly √3, at any contrast. Higher-energy modes can produce much larger overestimates. Rounding control adds a separate factor ρ/||r||₂. On known solutions we can measure the iterations each margin costs.

R^2 = (r^T r)/(mu r^T A'^(-1) r),     1 <= R <= sqrt(lambda_max(A')/mu).

A cheap observation can err in the other direction. Crouzeix’s theorem bounds the norm of a matrix function using the maximum absolute value of the corresponding scalar function on the matrix’s numerical range. Take the diagonal matrix from Section 5, with entries 1 and 10⁻⁸. Start a Krylov calculation in the second coordinate. Every power of the matrix keeps it there. The projected range contains only 10⁻⁸. For the identity function, substituting that range into the theorem would give an upper bound of 2 × 10⁻⁸ for a matrix whose norm is 1. The projection shows what the probe reached. A certificate also has to bound what it missed. [17, 66, 67]

The check must apply to the calculation that actually ran. In pipelined conjugate gradients the stored residual can look small while the residual of the returned iterate is still large. Cools and collaborators supplied a rule for selectively recomputing it. In a finite-element benchmark a joint NaN mask can drop a required location precisely when the prediction there is missing. Those two failures call for different repairs. Recompute against the original equations in the first case. Establish the required domain independently and check coverage in the second. Both repairs can be automated once the error has been identified. [33, 95]

Now suppose every recorded measurement is exact. A collaborator asks a question nobody had reason to ask when the data were saved. Can two physical fields, each consistent with the entire record, force different answers?


9. Which observations deserve to survive?

A collaborator wants one more answer from an exact record. Give each team the earlier runs, papers and freedom to choose what to save before the new question arrives. I specified numerical PDE and sumsets. Agents chose the two problems and worked out their answers. The comparison tells us where each kind of mathematics pays. The agents’ own work is also evidence about the competition. [151]

What a conservative mesh forgets

Let dye move at constant speed around a channel of unit circumference. Travel with the flow so the true concentrations stay put. Divide the channel into four equal cells. Fill alternate cells with dye, giving concentrations 1, 0, 1, 0. In a second run fill the other two. Shift the measurement grid by half a cell. Every new cell has one full half and one empty half. Both runs now report four averages of 1/2. Total mass is exactly right. Ask how much dye was in the first original cell. One answer is 1/4 and the other is zero. Any answer from the new averages alone is wrong by at least 1/8 on one run. [147, 151]

Which information should survive the next change of grid? On an even number n of equal cells the half-cell averages lose exactly one direction, the alternating pattern. A window covering two adjacent old cells gets its total from one shifted average. Every even-length window can be tiled by such pairs. For an odd number of whole old cells and concentrations between zero and one, the sharp worst-case error in the mass is 1/(2n). Compatible fields differ by a multiple of the alternating pattern. The two checkerboards attain the full ambiguity of 1/n, and the midpoint of the compatible range halves it. Saving one alternating sum restores every old cell average. It buys nothing for the even windows. [151]

Move the grid by a little more than half a cell. If the extra shift is a fraction d of a cell, with 0 < d < 1/2, the average of neighbors u and v becomes (1/2 − d)u + (1/2 + d)v. The alternating mode now survives with multiplier −2d. Recovering it amplifies an error in that mode by 1/(2d). An exact inverse can still be useless at the precision of the observations. Numerical analysis is an especially good home for that question because the receiving calculation can impose its own units, tolerance and cost. Karl Taylor gives a practical instance. In his coarse-grid temperature example, two remaps preserve the same global mean. Their area-weighted root-mean-square errors are 0.15 K and 0.63 K. The one with the smaller error also exceeds the source maximum. A collaborator who needs a bound on extremes has a real reason to demand another observation. [147, 153]

On an irregular mesh the underlying geometry is literal. Intersect each old cell with each new cell. If the concentration is constant inside each old cell, multiply that concentration by the overlap volume and sum to get the mass in a new cell. Farrell and Maddison use a common subdivision for conservative projection in two and three dimensions. Croci and Farrell prove that on two quasi-uniform meshes, with bounded subdivision per intersecting pair, the number of pieces grows at most linearly in the combined cell count. That prices the forward construction. It leaves open which regional masses the new measurements determine. [147, 158]

Brualdi and Ross asked which block totals survive when a binary matrix is replaced by its margins. On a mesh the overlap volumes are known and the old concentrations are the unknowns. Here the return question has an exact answer for one transfer. Write O for the matrix of overlap volumes and u for the old cell concentrations. The new masses are Ou. For a requested region, let w record how much of each old cell it covers. Its mass is the weighted sum of u with weights w. We can recover that mass for every old field exactly when w is a linear combination of the rows of O. The coefficients of that combination tell us how to add the new masses. If there is no such combination, some invisible perturbation changes the requested mass. Take small opposite multiples of it around half concentration and both fields remain between zero and one. They produce the same new masses. [164]

The coefficients also price the return trip. If each measured mass has absolute error at most δ, multiply δ by the sum of the absolute coefficients to bound the error those measurements pass on. An exactly recoverable region can demand more precision than the simulation stores. Accepting some error in the regional weights can buy a better guarantee from noisy readings. For one specified region, exact overlaps and independent interval bounds, a linear program gives the smallest possible worst-case error. Even a nonlinear reconstruction cannot beat it. [Appendix B](#optimal-regional-mass-estimate) proves this by exhibiting two donor fields with the same readings. The guarantee covers the represented field. A continuum claim also needs a bound on variation inside the donor cells. With several transfers, the maps compose after each intermediate mass is divided by its cell volume. Which observations are worth carrying forward before anyone knows which region will matter? [147, 151, 153]

What a complete sumset table forgets

Take the five marks 0, 1, 2, 6, 22 on a ruler. On a second ruler move only the 6 to 7. Form all sums of two marks, allowing repeats. The rulers agree on every labelled comparison, including the tie 0 + 2 = 1 + 1. Each has fourteen distinct sums. Add a third mark. On the first ruler 2 + 2 + 2 = 0 + 0 + 6. On the second, the corresponding sums separate. There are now 29 distinct sums on the first and 30 on the second. The full old table was a richer description than the count, and even it stopped one operation short. [145, 151]

Hide the inner marks. Tell the observer only the common two-sum order, the diameter 22, and that the five integer marks start at zero and have greatest common divisor one. Seven rulers fit. Their three-sum counts are 28, 29 or 30. Allow the observer to choose two labelled three-sums and ask whether they are equal. Four questions are necessary and sufficient in the worst case. Across the seven rulers the varying tests give only four different patterns of answers. Each singles out a different group with fewer than 30 sums. On a ruler with 30, all four answers are no, and each possible exception has to be excluded. Let the same question return less, equal or greater and two suffice. Here is the first signed comparison. Write the marks as 0, a, b, c, 22 and compare 3c with b + 22, padding with zero to make three summands. Equality settles the count at 29. In either strict branch a single further comparison settles it. The accompanying calculation checks all 595 pairs of labelled three-sums and both optimal worst-case bounds. The right to ask a sharper question has cut the required depth in half. [151]

The sumset successor has an exact demand of its own. In my preprint a face of a polytope records every labelled order and tie through a chosen number of summands. Its geometry distinguishes a new equality from a change of order. The three-sum count cares whether values merge. A change of order by itself can leave the count untouched. Which of the possible new equalities can change the count on a whole family of rulers? Keep five marks and increase the number of summands. Can the geometry of the old table bound the number of new signed comparisons needed for the next count? Which tables attain that bound? I have used the same habit in polynomial fibers. I wanted to count integer inputs x for which f(x) = g(y) has a rational lift beyond those supplied by polynomial composition. Given the effective curve operations in the paper, geometry and finite congruence tests decide whether that count has a power term. Listing every exceptional input would be a stronger demand. These papers establish that I can make this move. They are part of the rival team’s reading list as well. How much my presence would add is a separate question. [145, 146]

The machine rival belongs in this very field. Tim Gowers reports that both Isaac Rajagopal and ChatGPT discussed the prospect of achieving polynomial diameter. Gowers asked the model to pursue it. Rajagopal describes the mathematical brief as replacing his geometric progressions with smaller sets having the requisite pattern of sumset sizes. ChatGPT paired carefully chosen numbers with their multiples. The pairs create the wanted collisions while the generators exclude accidental short relations. Rajagopal worked out the bound for fixed sumset order and sufficiently many marks. The machine helped recognize the opportunity and invented the representation. In the two cases here, agents chose the examples, identified the lost observations and found the two-question sumset procedure. [145, 148, 151]

The rulers give the cleaner theorem about how many exact observations are necessary. The mesh gives the sharper question about the cost of reversing a loss at finite precision. A receiving model can specify a family of regions, the mass tolerance and the storage budget before it knows which region it will ask about. A proof that one affordable set of local measurements protects all those queries would change what a simulation can pass to its successor. A pair of indistinguishable fields that defeats every proposed set would change what the successor must request. I would do the mesh problem first. An analyst who works with the people inheriting those fields can bring the agent a question that would never show up in the remap’s own score. The proof then changes what the simulation records. [147, 151, 153]

Let the two agents question the receiving scientist too. If they keep finding the consequential missing observations as early and as cheaply as the partnership, I would give them the next mesh. On an open brief, let each team choose a successor problem. Exchange their proposals anonymously and have the other team pursue them. Someone who chooses a fruitful question can improve even a rival’s mathematics. [Appendix C](#experimental-protocol) sets out the comparison, including the sparse replay and the arithmetic screen. [89, 90, 105, 124, 132, 133, 134, 135, 137]


10. What could last after the discovery is public

The sharpest test is a theorem close enough to its predecessor to tempt you into copying the proof. In the Schiffer problem the outward derivative of an eigenfunction vanished on the boundary. The proof could discard certain harmonic coefficients. In the Berenstein problem that derivative was a nonzero constant. Those same coefficients now carried information needed to enforce the boundary condition. Colbrook, Sadeghi and Stepaniants found a candidate shape by Fourier search, moved to a fixed disk, kept the coefficients and bounded the omitted modes. They could certify a noncircular domain. [107, 108]

The researchers gave ChatGPT the earlier manuscript and its working code. The predecessor already contained the central construction, the fixed-disk formulation and the strategy for controlling the tail. The new paper credits ChatGPT with ideas for early versions of two proof steps and changes to the code. The authors developed and independently checked them. The old proof told them which coefficients they could throw away. The changed boundary condition made keeping those coefficients the decisive move. [107, 108]

On the next mesh I would ask the scientist who inherited the old runs which conclusion they could no longer draw. Work backward from that loss. Exhibit two fields the old measurements call identical. Find the observation that separates them at the precision the scientist needs. Publish that observation, its error bound and the code that measures it. Publish the cofactor recurrences and their certificate too. The two agents get to start the next problem with everything the mathematicians just taught them. Any lasting advantage has to survive that gift. [105, 130, 133, 134, 135, 145, 148, 151, 152, 163]

A mathematician can invent a method so good that nobody needs its inventor on the next hundred cases. That is a mathematical success. When the hundred-and-first case breaks the method, who finds the missing distinction, and what does it cost?

Appendix B. Solver evidence and extensions

Spectral comparison and approximate elimination

A constant vector has zero energy. For a connected graph this is the entire kernel. One can fix a vertex potential or work on the mean-zero subspace to obtain a positive-definite problem. A related class consists of symmetric diagonally dominant matrices with nonpositive off-diagonal entries, abbreviated SDDM here. Such a matrix has the form A = L + Gamma, where Gamma is nonnegative diagonal. It is positive definite when every connected component has a positive grounding term. The scope here is this concrete matrix class. [19, 26]

A preconditioned iteration repeatedly applies an inexpensive approximation to the inverse, aiming to reduce the total work needed to solve the original system. Write that action as solving with an auxiliary matrix M. For symmetric positive-definite A and M, suppose positive constants alpha and beta satisfy the following inequalities for every vector x.

alpha x^T M x <= x^T A x <= beta x^T M x.

Then the generalized eigenvalues of A relative to M lie between alpha and beta, and the corresponding condition number is at most beta/alpha. For a fixed positive-definite preconditioner in exact arithmetic, this ratio controls the standard worst-case convergence bound for conjugate gradients. A smaller condition number alone need not produce fewer iterations on a particular problem, since the spectral distribution matters. The computational benefit depends on convergence together with the cost of constructing and applying M. For Laplacians the comparison is made on the orthogonal complement of their common kernel. [52, 75]

Multigrid approaches the loss of information in another way. A cheap relaxation reduces some error components, and a coarse correction represents those that remain. Algebraically smooth error is error that the chosen relaxation struggles to remove. Its meaning depends on the operator and relaxation. Strongly connected regions joined by weak edges can move almost independently at low energy. A coarse representation must account for the resulting modes. The relevant objects are vectors, matrix blocks, graph partitions, and transfer operators. [20]

Refine the mesh in the [Poisson calculation](https://refactor.press/essays/continuity-as-organized-discrete-data#making-the-error-affordable-to-measure) and the zero function earns a smaller absolute Euclidean residual against the nodal test functions. Its relative residual stays at one. Its energy error against the continuum solution never improves. An agent rewarded for shrinking the absolute residual could beat its previous score every time without changing its answer. The missing ingredient is a bound connecting that particular measurement to the error we care about.

What one elimination leaves for the next

Gao, Kyng, and Spielman’s AC(2) samples interactions among the neighbors of an eliminated vertex and keeps the sample connected. It performs well on many symmetric diagonally dominant systems with nonpositive off-diagonal entries. The authors conjecture a relative condition number of O(log^2 m), possibly O(log m), with high probability for m input edges. The missing argument must control the random distortions as the Schur complement itself changes. [26]

A triangle shows why connectedness and the right average are insufficient. Start with unit weights. Choose one edge uniformly, give it weight 3 − 2ε, and give the other two weight ε, with 0 < ε < 1. Every outcome is connected and every edge still has expected weight one.

E[L_s] = L,     but kappa(L_s relative to L on the mean-zero subspace) = (2 - epsilon)/epsilon.

If edge 12 is heavy, (1,1,−2) and (1,−1,0) span the mean-zero subspace and have relative eigenvalues ε and 2 − ε. Every realization can therefore be a poor approximation although the average Laplacian is exact. This calculation isolates the gap between those two properties and a spectral guarantee. Actual elimination rules such as VAC and CAST use additional structure. [47, 48]

CAST lets us follow one such choice into the calculation. Increasing its sampling parameter tightens a bound on the local second moment. It also introduces edges that later eliminations may have to process. Extra edges stay cheap on some low-degree graphs, propagate expensively on Chimera systems and have less new fill to create inside already dense blocks. Pivot degree alone cannot price the choice. At the base setting, CAST-1 retained AC’s ordering, factor representation, and solver path. Over 250 right-hand sides its mean per-system speedup over AC was 1.17 on 17 SuiteSparse matrices and 1.18 on 128 Chimera systems. [48]

The five Chimera graphs expose two different ways to make a bad choice. On graph i₃, CAST-1 was 1.38 times faster at four right-hand sides per factor. At 250, CAST-2 was 1.17 times faster. The crossover lay between ten and fifty solves. The matrices came from Newton steps. The right-hand sides were synthetic Gaussian draws projected to mean zero. The reported times include setup and solves to a verified relative residual of 10⁻⁸. On the other four graphs, CAST-2 was still 1.3 to 4.6 times slower at 250. Knowing the number of solves can reverse the decision. Knowing that number alone cannot settle it. The authors could inspect the elimination profile after building a first factor. How cheaply could we detect the costly fill before paying for it? [48]

The four-cycle tells us exactly what a two-level correction must do. A recursive hierarchy needs its own analysis. An extra spatial dimension can also reopen a settled choice. A learned correction close to the exact coarse solve on a two-dimensional test needed appreciably more iterations than the exact solve on the finest three-dimensional grid. The comparison appears below under learning a component of a solver, alongside other methods for constructing and reusing coarse directions. [54, 78, 79, 85]

A small calculation can also locate a missing assumption in a proposed guarantee. APLICUR updates a low-rank preconditioner during an LSQR solve. In the authors’ Theorem 4.1 take the matrix and its rank-one CUR approximation both equal to diag(1,0), regularization μ = 1 and target t = 1/2. The preconditioned augmented matrix has singular values 1/2 and 1. The printed lower bound is 1, and its condition-number bound is √5/2. The actual condition number is 2. The approximation error here is zero. [87]

The point to repair is the retained block of the ideal Gram matrix. Its eigenvalue is t², while the omitted directions have eigenvalue μ². If μ ≤ t ≤ √(σ̂ℓ² + μ²), with σ̂ℓ the smallest retained singular value, and the CUR error E satisfies ||E||₂ < μ, the preconditioned singular values lie between μ − ||E||₂ and t + ||E||₂. The regularized default satisfies the added target restriction. Each phase still needs the approximation-error condition. Note 87 gives the calculation from the paper’s preconditioner. [87]

When checking dominates the cost

Saving a quarter of the iterations can buy almost nothing in wall time. On 1,000 AirfRANS systems the selected learned smoother of Chillón and collaborators converged on 99.9 percent. It used about 27 percent fewer V-cycles than relaxed Jacobi but saved only about 4 percent of solve time. The network predicted coefficients during setup. Applying the resulting sparse smoother cost more per cycle than diagonal Jacobi. AirfRANS ran in double precision because single precision stalled. On separate fine three-dimensional canonical-flow tests in single precision, convergence averaged 67.7 percent across ten trained models. The tolerance was 10^-4 and the cap was 500 cycles. Both the workload and the way a model was selected belong beside its performance figure. [40]

Shui and colleagues found lower mean latency for selected human-assisted GPU kernels than for autonomous artifacts on five matched B200 operator definitions, by factors of 1.35 to 13.25. The fastest top-k indexer passed a three-trial check and then failed three of 200 targeted trials. An earlier fallback passed 1,206 targeted trials. The other four definitions retained reported latency advantages. Different model choices, development budgets and supplied context leave the contribution of human guidance unresolved. [102]

For N comparable queries, include setup and the cost of diagnosing, solving and checking each one. Recovery includes the further attempts and fallback allowed by the declared timeout rules. Report remaining failures as well as expected time.

T(N) = T_setup + N (T_diagnose + T_solve + T_check + E[T_recovery]).

With extra setup cost ΔS and net saving d > 0 per query, expected break-even begins at N ≥ ΔS/d. Costs after a coefficient change can be correlated, so the mean needs an accompanying distribution. A diagnostic that takes several solves to evaluate can exhaust a four-solve job. A rule discovered once and reused on thousands of such jobs can still repay a long investigation. Each deployment pays for its own probes, checks and recovery. That distinction matters just as much for a proof. A criterion that excludes a family of covers can be cheap to apply forever. Its discovery is one research result. The next comparison starts with that result available to everybody. [48, 62, 86]

Accumulated error and coarse-space construction

Ballard, Demmel, Holtz and Schwartz prove communication lower bounds for specified dense and sparse linear-algebra algorithms under stated memory-access models. Reducing arithmetic can leave data movement expensive. A new representation may change the executed algorithm and thus the costs to which those bounds apply. [96]

Changed coefficients can move the difficult error to a new subspace, and reducing communication can amplify rounding error. A stalled iteration could reflect a missing coarse direction, an inaccurate inner solve or drift in the recursively computed residual. A fresh residual can reveal the last of these without constructing a second hierarchy. Established replacement rules already automate parts of that diagnosis. [20, 63, 95]

The Simons workshop report connects practical benchmarks with theoretical solver design. It asks for parameterized matrix families suitable for both practical evaluation and theoretical analysis, together with algorithms that are fast in both settings. Its multigrid problem combines an implicit inverse with uniform spectral control, near-linear construction, and linear application cost for a polynomially bounded subclass of the grounded SDDM matrices defined at the start of this appendix. Its nonnormal-solver questions seek bounds describing the early behaviour of GMRES. These targets require the guarantee, its construction cost, and its predictive value to be considered together. [75]

Related algorithms show how local accuracy can control a complete solve. Kyng, Lee, Peng, Sachdeva, and Spielman analyze Schur-complement chains in which the global spectral distortion is bounded by twice the sum of the local error tolerances, in their exponential comparison convention, while application work is charged across the levels. The chain must control both the approximate Schur complements and the inverses used for eliminated blocks. The result applies to a different construction under different assumptions. It makes error allocation precise by relating local tolerances to a global accuracy budget and the work charged across levels. [58]

VAC replaces an elimination clique by a sampled spanning tree with the target marginals, connected support, and efficient sequential and parallel construction. CAST adds a local minimax characterization and a tunable bound on the normalized local second moment. A full-factorization spectral bound would also need to control the way local errors accumulate. [47, 48]

The literature supplies concrete constructions and limitations. Constrained local approximate ideal restriction combines local transfer construction with constraints on selected modes and uses aggressive coarsening to keep hierarchies economical. Optimal-transfer theory characterizes best choices for wider nonsymmetric and indefinite settings. Sparse and inexpensive realization requires additional work. Algebraic Schwarz methods construct coarse information from local generalized eigenproblems. In Al Daas, Jolivet, Nataf, and Tournier's published analysis, the theorem concerns SPD matrices and its bound is not fully independent of the number of subdomains. Broader matrix classes appear in experiments. These distinctions identify the assumptions and costs a new construction would have to address. [49, 50, 51]

Finding and certifying an unknown domain

For the planar Berenstein conjecture the unknown is the domain itself. Can a bounded simply connected region other than a disk support a nonzero Laplace eigenfunction that vanishes on its boundary and has the same nonzero outward derivative everywhere there? Imposing both conditions makes the problem overdetermined. Colbrook, Sadeghi and Stepaniants searched for a shape using Fourier equations for boundary arclength. Those equations were good for finding a candidate and poor for bounding every omitted mode. To prove existence they pulled the problem back to a fixed disk and changed coordinates. Their estimates certify a noncircular domain with analytic boundary and a sign-changing eigenfunction. [107]

One detail explains why the earlier Schiffer proof could not simply be rerun. There the outward derivative vanished, which allowed certain harmonic coefficients to be eliminated. Berenstein’s nonzero derivative carried information in precisely those coefficients. The team kept them, coupled the interior and boundary equations, bounded the infinite tail, and recovered the required sign after squaring the boundary condition. ChatGPT received the predecessor manuscript and working code. The authors credit it with ideas for early versions of two proof steps and changes to the code, which they developed and checked. Reusing the framework meant discovering which part of it had ceased to be true. [107, 108]

There is a second route to the Schiffer result itself. Cao-Labora and de Dios Pont let the integer order of rotational symmetry vary as a real parameter. Most of those values describe an auxiliary problem. Uniform estimates let them follow bifurcating branches far enough to reach actual integers, yielding infinitely many noncircular domains. The first proof certifies an individual shape. The second creates room between symmetry orders in which a whole family becomes accessible. The authors of the computer-assisted Schiffer proof credit AI with debugging early code. In the second, models checked asymptotics numerically and drafted arguments for Bessel-function estimates. Even with the conjecture held fixed, the mathematical invention and the division of labor changed together. [108, 111]

From a finite system to a PDE guarantee

Differential equations supply important sparse matrices, but the initial solver problem can be specified by the matrix, the right-hand side, and the required error measure. Graph incidence and finite matrix inequalities are enough to state it. Sobolev spaces and homological language are not universal prerequisites for this programme. A claim of accuracy for a continuous PDE also needs a valid approximation argument. For smooth finite-difference problems this can use Taylor expansion and a discrete maximum principle. More general settings benefit from other tools. [53]

Compatible vector discretizations provide a demanding later application. Their edge and face incidences constrain the transfers, and their coefficient weights determine the energies. Shen and collaborators' three-dimensional H(curl) construction leaves a sparse multilevel realization open. Tuminaro and Glusa supply a different sparse construction with commuting constraints. The two papers vary coefficients in different terms, so one does not settle the other's robustness question. Auxiliary-space and GenEO methods remain substantive alternatives. Arnold, Falk and Winther require a discrete subcomplex and uniformly bounded commuting projections for their mixed Hodge stability result. Their square-mesh Maxwell example makes the danger concrete. On a crisscross mesh the first seven nonzero computed eigenvalues converge to genuine eigenvalues. The eighth converges to a spurious one. Further refinement can make the wrong answer look more convincing. [22, 23, 24, 25, 43]

A PDE application introduces an additional error between the continuous solution and the exact solution of the discrete system. To balance these two errors under mesh refinement, the solver needs a stopping tolerance suited to the current mesh. Certified reduced-basis methods and adaptive finite-element estimators address that further layer under explicit assumptions. Yano's complementary-energy construction, for example, requires symmetric coercivity, affine parameter dependence, and specialized offline work. The p-robust adaptive analysis cited here has a computable contraction criterion and a stated Poisson setting. These are application-specific extensions, not prerequisites for studying the finite matrix algorithm. [27, 46]

Van den Berg and Breden get an effective global existence result for the one-dimensional Ohta-Kawasaki equation. Their coefficients approximate one trajectory from prescribed initial data. Bounds on the omitted modes validate successive intervals and a final step covering all future time. For the parameters and initial data in Theorem 1.2, the exact trajectory stays within 9 × 10⁻⁷ of the approximation, uniformly in space for every t ≥ 0. Staying in a narrow tube would still permit motion inside it forever. The final step also certifies a stable stationary solution and a neighborhood in which trajectories converge to it. That stability argument supplies the long-time conclusion. On a separate Swift-Hohenberg trajectory, their change of validation method made the proof about 25 times faster on the reported hardware. [109]

Agent search and software optimization

Coding, benchmarking, and revising a solver are already being automated. Bader and collaborators report agents improving the tuned VieCut minimum-cut implementation by factors of 1.28 sequentially and 1.63 at 32 threads on the real-world k-core workload. All 23 accepted edits received human review, and the same 53-instance workload was used for threshold tuning and reported timing. This is productive algorithm engineering under the stated conditions. [28]

FrontierCS 2 brings that competition into recent research code. Its 4 November 18-system, 48-task snapshot includes 13 systems passing Clusterwise SpGEMM, eight passing the KAMI numerical-kernel task, and seven passing Polar Express. Each task uses its own correctness and performance criteria. They are nevertheless actual successes in sparse and numerical algorithm engineering. [69]

ADSD provides a more targeted comparison. Across nine model/task comparisons, direct revision worsens error in five, whereas its combined diagnosis, literature-retrieval, and skill pipeline reports lower error throughout. Five rounds of direct revision on GOC-500 do not close the gap. This combined automated procedure can improve the tested searches beyond simply requesting another attempt. The ablation assesses diagnosis, retrieval, and skill construction together. Off-the-shelf solvers are excluded, and matching the final revision budget does not equalize the cost of constructing the pipeline. The result establishes a useful automated mechanism whose costs should be included in a comparison with specialist-led work. On the optimal-power-flow task the agent used the failure traces to ask how first-order quantities should be put on comparable scales. Its next diagnostic question arose inside the supplied optimization goal. [30]

AlphaEvolve can search over constructors and algorithms and use multiple evaluation scores, including expensive accelerator measurements. TTT-Discover updates model weights while working on the current problem, using feedback from earlier attempts. Its reported method targets one high-performing result under a continuous reward, with applications in mathematical bounds, GPU kernels, and algorithm engineering. Transfer of the learned procedure to further problems is a separate question. A comparison should include agents that learn during the investigation. [44, 88]

Learning a component of a solver

A research agent can discover a classical algorithm that uses no model when deployed. A neural component can predict a coarse space, a sparse correction, or a tuning decision. A surrogate can approximate a solution map. These are different mechanisms. For the finite programme, the central question is whether the resulting factor or hierarchy solves the original system at the required accuracy and total cost.

Sparse approximate inverses illustrate why representation design and hardware belong in the same argument. Yang and collaborators replace triangular applications with sparse matrix-vector products and train the admissible entries. Their comparisons change across matrix families and CPU/GPU implementations. Default multigrid remains strong on diffusion examples. The research decision concerns which representation preserves enough spectral information at the cost the hardware can actually deliver. [77]

Melchers, Dolean, and Abdelmalik compare four neural-operator coarse corrections while fixing the output coarse space. They vary input discretization and linear dependence on the right-hand side. Preserving right-hand-side linearity and matching the input projection to the output basis give the Neural Green's Operator a Galerkin-type coarse correction. The experiments connect these choices to spectral behaviour, stagnation, and compatibility with Krylov solvers. Their November revision adds a three-dimensional diffusion test. On the finest grid, with 80 points per side, the learned correction took 37.3 flexible-GMRES iterations on average. The exact coarse solve took 23.0 with the same smoother, and the smoother alone took 124.1. The learned correction still helped substantially. Its near parity with the exact coarse solve on the two-dimensional test did not carry over. These counts tell us what to investigate before trusting the architecture on another grid. [85]

Bao, Cui, and Li learn coarse spaces from errors left behind by smoothing. Their objective compares subspaces independently of the basis used to express them. A perturbation analysis relates the discrepancy, including pre- and postsmoothing, to the resulting two-grid operator. The sharper quantity has a small Gram-matrix representation, but forming it requires solves with the original matrix, so it is used offline. The convergence bounds are conditional and the experiments concern stated parameterized families. The work exposes a concrete next problem of finding an informative, affordable test for when to reuse, enrich, or rebuild the learned space. [86]

RAPNet learns sparse corrections to coarse and transfer operators within an algebraic multigrid hierarchy, with transfer from training subgraphs of about 4,000 nodes to million-node tests. For some graph families the training patches come from large graphs to expose relevant structures. Its setup comparison uses classical CPU execution and GPU RAPNet, counting transfers and inference. A general convergence guarantee for unrestricted corrections remains open. These results demonstrate meaningful transfer, with its scope set by the training graphs, hardware, and stated limitations. [37]

AutoSpec learns short spectral recurrences, producing matrix polynomials for approximation, preconditioning, and filtering. Its comparisons include Chebyshev preconditioners supplied with the same spectral estimates and show both wins and losses. The tested operators are positive definite after the stated transformations. Some originally indefinite eigenproblems are squared, and some difficult linear systems also use incomplete Cholesky. Those details are part of what the results mean. Learning can thus return a compact executable numerical procedure with recognizable operations. An iteration-count improvement still needs a separate account of probe cost, training, and application time before becoming an end-to-end speedup. [38]

PCGBandit selects among 33 fixed preconditioner configurations during one OpenFOAM run and counts learning and backstop costs. In the Shercliff electric-potential solves, expensive early trials can absorb the savings of short runs. He and collaborators report total-runtime reductions of 36.1-43.3 percent on diffusion and 19.8-31.0 percent on diffusion-advection against the BoomerAMG reference default over 5,000-system sequences. In a separate same-hierarchy test on SPD diffusion, the mean first-attempt solve time favored a prescribed two-weight schedule by 2.8 percent, with a reported spread of 3.8 percent across six frozen learned states, whereas the same schedule failed frequently on variable-flow diffusion-advection. The strength of the comparator changes with the problem. [39, 62]

Precision, storage and the cost of a solve

Fast algebra and numerical stability can also be reconciled. Demmel, Dumitriu, and Holtz give normwise-stable algorithms for standard dense linear-algebra operations with essentially the matrix-multiplication exponent, under their stated arithmetic and stability assumptions. That result neither preserves arbitrary sparse structure nor establishes practical constants. A sparse implementation still needs control of its structure, accuracy, and application cost. [60]

The OpenAI release also reports constructions with explicit implementation-cost accounting. OpenAI's manuscript on exact Fourier transforms reports an asymptotic operation bound below n log n while explicitly accounting for scalar preparation, schedule construction, and indexing. Its construction preserves working width and restores borrowed registers. The result assumes exact complex arithmetic, supplied roots of unity, and unrestricted coefficients. A floating-point realization would need its own stability analysis. The proposed architecture reaches well beyond a bare existence theorem. [56] A separate OpenAI manuscript on integer multiplication explicitly propagates fixed-point errors and bounds the final coefficient error below one half so that rounding recovers the exact answer. Its asymptotic bit-complexity result comes with enormous constants, not a useful practical crossover. These are reported constructions, not independently certified here. These reports put finite-precision analysis among the tasks to include in a comparison with research agents. [57]

Arithmetic precision is another resource to allocate. McCormick, Benzaken, and Tamstorf develop a purely algebraic mixed-precision multigrid analysis for sparse symmetric positive-definite systems. Residual evaluation, correction solves, and iterate updates contribute differently to the energy-error bound, and precision can vary across levels. This supplies an established framework for spending precision where it matters. Better adaptive rules must respect the convergence and rounding assumptions, as well as the costs of storing and applying the sparse operators. [59]

Jang, Jolivet, and Mary identify a structural failure in mixed-precision GMRES-DR. Its derivation uses simplified relations from Arnoldi iteration and orthogonality whose finite-precision defects can make the method stagnate. Returning to a more general augmented formulation permits lower-precision inner work without those simplifications. The experiments retain precision-sensitive cases and defer hardware-specific timing. The example links a diagnosis to a mathematical repair of the formulation. [63]

A more focused question is whether the memory saved by compressing a preconditioner outweighs the cost of extra iterations. Khan and Carson report a steam1 case with 21 GMRES iterations against 14 in uniform precision, while storage falls to 74.9 or 42.6 percent of the uniform version under two thresholds. Those figures leave runtime open because reduced traffic competes with extra iterations and other work. The authors leave predicting favorable matrices as further research. Meanwhile doubly adaptive GMRES already combines iteration-dependent and entry-dependent precision automatically, with a reported peak 1.6-fold speedup on nd24k. Figure 6 compares runs that attained the baseline backward error. Table 3 records stagnation on several matrices and shows that one heuristic attained the target on only one matrix. The failures belong in the comparison. The research target is a better, transferable prediction of when these precision choices repay their cost. [64, 65]

The distinction between a research agent and a solution surrogate also matters economically. Huang and Stuart's comparisons of neural operators with classical solvers depend on accuracy and on amortized training and data costs. The plateau they observe for particular Darcy surrogates is not evidence that an agent cannot discover a better sparse solver. For the present programme, the relevant comparison is the final matrix algorithm, its construction costs, and the workload over which those costs are recovered. [41]

Zhang, Li, and Saad tested a learned multilevel preconditioner on 867 real non-SPD SuiteSparse matrices. Median setup/training time was 135.77 seconds versus 0.18 for the tested AMG. Median solve time also favored AMG, 1.31 seconds versus 8.08. Mean solve time favored the learned method, 9.17 seconds versus 50.87. Its convergence win shares are conditional on valid runs. Separate medians cannot be added to recover paired total times. Cross-matrix transfer and the tails of the cost distribution both matter when choosing the deployment workload. [42]

Nonnormal operators and verified sparse solving

Crouzeix points to an adjacent programme in nonnormal matrix computation. For a function f holomorphic near the numerical range W(A), the reported resolution gives the following bound.

||f(A)||_2 <= 2 max_{z in W(A)} |f(z)|.

For normal matrices the spectral theorem already supplies a constant-one bound. The new direction concerns operators for which eigenvalues alone give an incomplete account, including nonsymmetric systems and some directed-graph problems. Crouzeix and Greenbaum use the new result to improve bounds on other spectral regions and apply them to GMRES and rational Krylov methods. Choosing a useful region, approximation, and inner-solve accuracy is a further design problem. A numerical-range bound containing zero can fail to certify GMRES contraction. A valid cutout that excludes zero without surrounding it may help, but requires additional information about the inverse. [66]

The direction of the approximation matters when estimating a spectral region. For an orthonormal Krylov basis V, W(V* A V) lies inside W(A). This projected set is an inner approximation, while a certificate based on an outer enclosure needs a bound in the other direction. An ordinary largest Ritz value of a Hermitian support matrix is a lower estimate. An outer support line needs an upper bound. Chen and Urschel quantify Krylov approximation of numerical ranges and its limitations in exact arithmetic. A floating-point certificate requires further control. This identifies the information a cheap diagnostic must obtain before its estimate can support a rigorous guarantee. [67]

Verified sparse solving also has established automatic methods. Rump develops entrywise error bounds for real and complex sparse systems, using inexpensive bounds first and stronger residual-based work when needed. Different factorizations and fill patterns can change verification cost sharply. A new certificate must therefore improve an existing procedure, or apply where its assumptions fail, rather than claim that rigorous checking has not yet been automated. [68]

What autonomous solver research already achieves

LinearSolveBench released sparse solvers selected from 640 candidates per model on 96 magnetic-diffusion cases. The best passed the specified checks on all 96 and ran about 1.85 times faster than GMRES(50) with BoomerAMG when the original case times were summed. The timed work covered solver creation, setup and solution. Discovery, compilation and independent verification were outside that clock. The release adds a fresh correctness check on the same cases. Speed on a new family is an open question. [32]

An agent also gets to use the adaptive solvers that already exist. PCGBandit tries configurations from a menu of 33 during a flow simulation and charges for the trials and the fallback. The Shercliff simulation made 7,500 pressure solves. With the reference incomplete-Cholesky preconditioner those solves consumed 59 percent of the runtime. There is a bill worth cutting, and the learner improved the pressure solves. On the electric-potential solves in the same simulation, its costly early choices swallowed much of what it could save later. Someone who claims to have invented a better diagnostic has a working competitor to beat. [62]

What happens to a human contribution once it can be written down? In a finite-element task in SciCode-Verified, agents kept replacing the specified weak form with its textbook counterpart. Restating the equations at implementation time corrected that choice. Across 64 corrected main problems, expert background raised GPT-5.5 from 65.6 to 90.6 percent, Gemini 3.5 Flash from 51.6 to 87.5 percent, and Claude Opus 4.8 from 57.8 to 78.1 percent. Each model ran once per condition. Every scored subproblem had to pass, and it could pass in either of two software environments. Writing the background and independently rediscovering it were both unpriced. Once every team receives it, a later gain from using it cannot be credited again to a specialist’s presence. [35]

The autonomous competitor can also question its judge. During AIDE²’s eight-day research run an agent found that one failing test case could crash the scoring of the entire set. It repaired the scoring script. Seven harness changes were accepted. The target suite and outer objective were supplied, but no operator had named that repair. A machine can choose a new diagnostic project inside an existing programme. [55]

In SVG-EAR, Astra wrote a probe that periodically compared sparse attention with exact attention on a small sample of queries. Its code assigned a budget to each head using the measured error and fell back to dense attention when necessary. Astra also supplied a variance correction. Two additional agents independently rediscovered that correction in exploratory runs. The final code reduced mean runtime by about 18 percent, improved all three mean fidelity measures and passed 43 of 48 hidden checks. None of the twelve evaluated agents passed the whole task. The study assessed the combined changes. The five fidelity failures also occurred in a separate exact-attention implementation. An agent had devised an informative measurement and used it to change the calculation. [70]

Lu and colleagues hid which equation and coefficients generated a field, supplied sparse measurements and a library of possible equations, and tested the resulting solver on held-out cases. On 278 feasibility-screened cases with unseen templates or shifted coefficients, iterative Codex with GPT-6.1 passed 261. A single-run baseline passed 135. The researchers had specified the field accuracy to achieve. Whether an agent would discover that a different physical quantity needed protecting is the next question. [157]

Chow and Li developed two adaptive solvers for electronic-structure calculations, one through an autonomous coding system and one by iterating with Codex themselves. On two difficult iron complexes both implementations detected a plateau in the orbital-gradient norm and recovered. On the easy serine peptide both reached the same solution. The human-guided implementation also ran a stability analysis after convergence and took about six times as long as the standard iteration. The autonomous implementation took about as long as the standard one. The development tools differed, and a different calculation may require the extra check. On the nickel complex both adaptive solvers reached internally stable states of slightly higher energy than the standalone orbital trust-region method. Detecting a plateau, verifying internal stability and identifying the physical ground state are three different claims. [114]

A flow simulation can also finish successfully while supplying the wrong evidence for its conclusion. Somasekharan and colleagues added a visual check to solver logs. A model reading the rendered fields flagged 14 of 16 planted failures. It also flagged all four clean controls. The test harness had omitted plots requested by its own instructions. Give a second agent a different view and it can catch mistakes. Give it an incomplete view and it can veto good work. The skill at issue includes deciding when a missing observation calls for a new calculation and when it calls for opening the rest of the file. Separate notebooks and disciplinary perspectives have helped machine teams find better architectures. The human rival must earn the same credit through what the extra perspective changes. [128, 154]

The best possible regional mass estimate

Use the single-transfer model in Section 9. Each donor concentration lies between zero and one, the overlap matrix O is exact, and each reported receiver mass differs from its true value by at most δ. Subtract the known masses of the uniform half-concentration field. Estimate the requested regional mass by taking a weighted sum of these centered readings with coefficients c and adding back the known half-concentration mass of the region. A donor concentration now differs from its center by at most one half. The triangle inequality gives the following guarantee.

absolute mass error <= (1/2) ||w - O^T c||_1 + delta ||c||_1.

Here the 1-norm is the sum of absolute entries. The first term prices the part of the regional weights the chosen overlaps fail to represent. The second prices the measurement noise the chosen coefficients can amplify. Both terms are in units of mass. With independent interval bounds on concentrations and noise, choosing their signs to agree with the respective coefficients attains the bound in the worst case for this estimator.

Minimize that bound over all choices of c. Linear-programming duality identifies its minimum with the largest regional mass change w · z among perturbations z whose entries have magnitude at most one half and whose receiver mass changes Oz have magnitude at most δ in each entry. Take a maximizing z. Add it to the uniform half-concentration field or subtract it. In each case admissible noise can cancel the change at every receiver. The two fields yield identical readings, yet their regional masses differ by twice w · z. Any answer from those readings must miss at least one of them by w · z or more. The minimizing linear estimate attains that worst-case error, even among nonlinear estimates. An extra stored measurement adds a row to O and may lower the optimum. A bound for the underlying continuum field still requires information about variation inside the cells. [147, 151, 158]


Appendix C. Experimental protocol

Solver implementations and held-out tests

Before the first trial, record the implementation and version, precision, hardware, smoother, coarsening and coarse solve, grid sizes, μ, weight ranges, change generators, budgets and stopping rules. Each preconditioned-CG run requires a fixed linear symmetric positive-definite applied cycle. The three policies respectively keep the old hierarchy intact (F), update its level matrices and smoothers while retaining transfers (U), or rebuild it (R). To certify F, compare the changed weights with the initial weights and use proved α and β for the actual baseline. If only spectral estimates are available, report the policy comparison as empirical. The independent μ-based residual check can still certify answers under all three policies. Acceptance under U supplies no uniform quality bound on the updated hierarchy.

Begin with manufactured solutions normalized to unit A'-energy, including directions aligned with the modes missed after a weight change, localized patterns, and generic patterns. Form their right-hand sides consistently and account for construction error. Normalization gives the prescribed absolute energy tolerance a comparable scale across the controlled tests. Then test unknown solutions with a declared right-hand-side normalization and the same prescribed absolute energy tolerance τ. Use four right-hand sides as the primary short workload, with one and a prespecified longer horizon as sensitivity cases. Hold out coefficient-change regimes and larger sizes.

Keep manufactured reference solutions and exact-error measurements within the independent checker, outside the information supplied to the candidate solver or policy selector. On reference cases, record the first iteration at which the actual energy error and the certified bound each reach the prescribed tolerance. Their difference counts the additional iterations associated with the conservative stopping rule. Measure the corresponding solve time and deployed checking cost separately. Report inflation from validated residual evaluation separately.

Obtain reference-error measurements in separate calibration runs. Match the timed runs' inputs, initial states, arithmetic, random seeds, and algorithmic decisions, and compare their residual histories and iteration counts. If the trajectories diverge, attribute the calibration to its own trajectory. Keep the deployed acceptance checker active in timed runs and charge it to solution cost. Account for reference instrumentation and replay under research and validation, identifying shared benchmark costs and costs incurred by each group.

Time each selector and each fixed policy on paired workloads. Charge transfers, updates, coarse factorizations, setup, solves and deployed independent checks. On failed acceptance, rebuild and retry within a fixed budget, then count unresolved cases. Before evaluation, fix the worthwhile cost-improvement margin, uncertainty rule and allowable failure rate. Report paired total times and outcome distributions at the same accuracy for each workload horizon.

An elimination project can use AC(2) with a fixed implementation, including its arithmetic, randomization, and input class. Compare any repair with unmodified AC(2), approximate-elimination alternatives such as CAST, and strong multigrid or direct-solver baselines for the declared workload. Reserve graph families as well as seeds, including weakly joined clusters, long narrow connections, graded weights, degree variation, and larger instances. Report fill, peak memory, setup, factor application, convergence, independent error checks, failures, and fallback costs. Separate a single unfamiliar matrix, a short sequence after a structural change, and a long related sequence. A diagnosis should predict which regime repays its cost. [26, 48, 62]

Broader coarse-space work should include adaptive smoothed aggregation, compatible-relaxation or bootstrap constructions, and hierarchy-reuse policies on a declared SPD family. Compare with online configuration methods and well-chosen fixed settings, not only library defaults. On manageable sizes include an expensive spectral reference. Charge candidate generation, probes, local eigensolves, rejected attempts, rebuilding, and checking. Measure coarse dimension, transfer nonzeros, hierarchy size, memory, and total time. All groups may change support or aggregation and invent alternatives. An exact-coarse-solve theorem must be assessed separately from its recursive implementation. [20, 39, 54, 76, 78, 79]

A high-probability theorem must state its input assumptions, randomization, and failure-probability dependence. Practical predictions also require the relevant constants. Keep exact small-instance calculations, large-instance estimates, and validated proofs distinct. Independently recompute residuals against the original operator and check the declared kernel and positivity assumptions.

Across these projects, retain final evaluation cases outside development and record exploratory discoveries separately. Assess a proposed checker correction against the original mathematical requirement. An added application requirement defines a further task. [45]

Reproducing the two pilot calculations

Start the solver pilot with a shifted grid Laplacian A(w) = L(w) + μ I, positive edge weights and fixed μ > 0. Build an initial multigrid hierarchy, change the weights once, and solve the changed system A′x = b. Every trial starts with the same initial hierarchy for its operator. A selector has three choices. [76, 78, 79]

Three responses to the same change in edge weights.
Policy | Operation after a weight change
F (fixed) | Retain the old preconditioner in full, including every level matrix, smoother, transfer and coarse factorization.
U (updated) | Retain the transfers. Recompute the Galerkin operators, smoothers and coarse factorization.
R (rebuilt) | Build a new hierarchy with the original setup procedure.

Only F keeps the preconditioner covered by the weight-ratio argument in Section 6. Check every policy against the same independent energy-error bound from Section 8. Before designing a selector, find out what choosing well could buy. On development jobs, replay F, U and R from the same saved hierarchy. Repeat paired timings so the apparent saving does not depend on picking a lucky run. Give hindsight all the replay results. Let it pick one action per job to minimize total time while meeting exactly the accuracy and reliability rules the live selector must meet. Charge setup, checking, recovery and failed runs alike. If every job must pass, this reduces to picking the cheapest successful action on each. If no assignment meets the rules, even foreknowledge fails. Compare this free hindsight choice with the fastest eligible baseline, counting both fixed policies and the existing adaptive policy. The gap is a ceiling on what choosing among F, U and R could save on that batch before paying for information.

The published CAST timings let us put a number on that ceiling for a narrower choice. CAST reports 28, 24, 27, 26 and 23 systems on its five Chimera graphs. At 250 right-hand sides, weighting the published graph means by those counts gives about 2,313 seconds for CAST-1 throughout and 2,251 if hindsight chooses one of the two CAST variants per graph. The entire saving comes from the third graph. Its 27 systems save about 2.3 seconds apiece. The displayed numbers are rounded means of per-system medians over five factor draws. A policy choosing separately for every matrix could have a different ceiling. On the bench we can price this particular choice at about 62 seconds per batch before paying for a probe. On a deployment we need its matrices, right-hand sides, reuse horizon and best existing policy. [48, 120]

The same distinction shows up on an eight-vertex cycle small enough for exact arithmetic. Across four changes of weights, four right-hand sides apiece, F, U and R took 65, 48 and 46 conjugate-gradient steps. Picking the fewest steps on each job also took 46. A selector earned nothing on that score. Setup could still make updating cheaper than rebuilding. Rasouli and collaborators actually timed related updates after mesh deformation. On their stretched mesh a fine-level update beat rebuilding through the ninth case, and rebuilding paid on the tenth. Their fine-level policy updated the operator. Our fixed policy retains the old preconditioner in full. Replaying these choices is established practice. Once these comparisons are public, the agent can run them too. Recognizing a documented crossover on the original workload supplies no evidence of a continuing centaur advantage. [76, 120]

The arithmetic replication starts from a different demand. Can an irreducible degree-23 polynomial over the rationals have all twenty-three roots real and Galois group M₂₃? Huang and collaborators solved the problem without the condition on the roots. Even the branch data for their construction took work to choose. Seven complex covers had the chosen branching. Galois conjugation permuted six and fixed one. They found equations over the rationals for that one. Forty-digit expansions and a change of scale recovered its equations. For the other covers even sixty digits left some coefficients unrecognizable. The team combined data from the six other permutation triples and changed scale again. The authors describe AI-assisted computation under human direction. They independently checked the final claims in Magma and PARI/GP without AI. [110]

Try the published polynomial on the new demand. An exact Sturm count gives seven real roots. The other sixteen form eight conjugate pairs. Choosing another rational parameter cannot fix it. The real parameter line, with infinity included, stays connected after its only real branch point is removed. Complex conjugation keeps the same action away from that point, and the authors checked the exceptional point separately. A stronger result was already on the shelf. Fried and Dèbes exclude a full-group totally real specialization of any regular three-branch-point M₂₃ Galois cover over the rationals. In the relevant three-point case the group would have to be generated by two involutions and would be dihedral. M₂₃ is not. Malle’s constructions for M₁₁ and M₁₂ show why trying a different family is serious mathematics. Give every team these results before the clock starts. Reading a known exclusion can save work. Rediscovering it earns no extra credit. [110, 116, 118]

Here is a fresh structural pilot. Try four ordered rational branch points, an interval where all the sheets are real, and a genus-zero source curve so its equations might be manageable. Cap the four branching orders at four. Before reconstructing anything, enumerate the permutations that could describe such a cover. In the natural action of M₂₃ on 23 letters the resulting test reduces to four permutations (a, ab, bc, c), where a, b and c are involutions and ab and bc have order four. Their product is automatically the identity. They must also generate the full group. Counting up to simultaneous conjugation in M₂₃ gives 480 candidate classes. Exactly 16 generate M₂₃. An independent orbit count gives the same answer. Anyone planning to recover equations for a rejected class can stop immediately. [118, 120]

The sixteen survivors do not settle the arithmetic. Each describes a complex cover whose degree-23 source curve has genus zero. The sixteen generating classes in the restricted screen lie in a full braid orbit of 2,456 generating classes. The curve that parametrizes this orbit is a different object. Häfner computed genus 447 for its ordered parameter curve. The finite screen has identified where a rational point would have to be sought in the appropriate real region. It has produced no such point. The next arithmetic question is whether the relevant part of that curve admits a rational point. Finding an obstruction there could retire the route. Passing the finite group test alone gives none of the sixteen candidates equations over the rationals. [119, 120]

On the eight-cycle, consecutive edge weights alternate between 1 and 1/64 and the diagonal shift is 1/16. Rescale all weights by 5/4, exchange the two parities, raise the original strong weights to 4, or set every weight to 1. The rebuilt hierarchy pairs adjacent vertices along the heavier parity, retaining the original pairing on a tie. Its two-level preconditioner uses one damped Jacobi sweep on each side of an exact Galerkin correction. Fine PCG starts at zero and checks the original residual against an absolute energy-error tolerance of 10⁻⁴. On the exchange alone, F, U and R took 18, 13 and 11 total steps across the four right-hand sides. The supplied code specifies the vectors, reruns all policies with rational arithmetic and separately reconstructs the graph-level CAST comparison from Table 5. [48, 120]

For the M₂₃ screen the branch index is 23 minus the number of cycles, fixed letters included. Genus zero requires the four indices to sum to 44. The two end involutions contribute 8 apiece. Under the order cap the two middle permutations must each have order four and index 14. Fix the middle involution b. The 1,008 possible endpoints split into three orbits under its centralizer, which has order 2,688. For each left representative take the right orbits under its stabilizer. This gives 480 ordered classes. Burnside’s lemma independently counts the same orbits. There are 43,008 transitive labeled pairs for fixed b. Every such triple generates M₂₃ and has trivial stabilizer, yielding 43,008/2,688 = 16. The script prints a witness, verifies the branch product, and checks rationality of its conjugacy classes. A rational class condition alone cannot supply equations over the rationals. [110, 118, 119, 120]

Comparing teams on the same brief

What would establish each claim?
Claim | Required evidence
Better performance on a stated workload | Independent measurements at the same accuracy, including setup, checking, and recovery.
Transfer beyond development cases | Withheld families with meaningful changes in geometry, coefficients, topology, or other relevant structure.
A uniform mathematical guarantee | A valid argument covering the stated class, with its assumptions and parameter dependence explicit.

Decide in advance when an obstruction answers the question. Suppose a team proves that a specified family of cheap observations gives identical answers on two allowed changes that require different actions to meet the cost and accuracy target. That can settle whether the proposed policy class will suffice. In a cover project, a proof excluding the prescribed class of realizations earns the corresponding credit if its scope settles the declared question. A stalled search earns neither verdict. Flag in advance which briefs admit a positive construction, a decisive obstruction, or both. Report the two kinds of result separately as well as success under each brief. In the four-solve performance pilot the positive outcome still requires a faster accepted method. Evidence for stopping a search and any demonstrable work it saves get their own record.

Hold the lead agent fixed. Give one team a specialist. Give the other a second agent chosen on separate development problems, free to use a different model, tools or proof strategy. Each gets the same starting evidence, access to the same tools and the same total budget. Also run the lead on its own with the resources available for extra attempts. Let C_H and C_A be the total research costs at which the human-agent pair and the machine pair respectively first produce an independently accepted result. Count advice, checking and failed searches. Assign infinite cost to a run that exhausts its protocol without an accepted result. At budget b the human partner’s advantage is

Delta(b) = Pr(C_H <= b) - Pr(C_A <= b).

At three hours Archon’s run with the human blueprint had finished and the other run had not. At four hours both had finished. Repeat the partner swap across fresh problems. Report correct rescues and harmful vetoes as well as the net gain. A generalist paired with the lead tests whether the gain requires subject expertise. A specialist working with the same software and sources without generative AI tests whether the machine helps the person too. Let the strongest machine-only procedure found in development compete for the same budget, even if it uses parallel independent searches instead of a pair in conversation. [90, 103, 104, 123, 124]

In the open-ended transfer experiment, the unit of comparison is a portfolio of follow-up problems selected from the same precursor and historical record. Predeclare time and research costs, eligibility for a new claim, the process for checking originality, and how independent assessors will judge mathematical substance. Keep the proposed question and success criterion on record before its result is known. Report questions proposed, questions abandoned, verified results and assessor disagreement. Separate value attributable to selecting the problem from value attributable to solving a named one. Include previously unanticipated follow-ups that meet the criteria. [4, 132, 133]

A human can help a model because their errors overlap less, and lose that benefit by deferring to it at the wrong time. Steyvers and colleagues combined independent image judgments and confidence scores under the same fitted rule. Human-machine pairs gained most over pairs of different networks when the partners had comparable accuracy and the images were noisy. Hemmer and colleagues instead held human participation fixed. On fifteen images, changing which classifier advised participants lowered mean team error from 24.7 to 14.6 percent although both classifiers missed four images alone. AutoAnalysis reports that in its subset comparing collaborations with both solo conditions, average team performance fell below the stronger individual condition. None of these designs asks who will invent a missing mathematical test. [103, 104, 129]

For each portfolio, define the project sampling frame, intended population, acceptance rules and distribution of briefs before execution. Classify projects by the deliverable specified at entry. Fix the treatment of genuinely mixed briefs and pair analysis by project. A construction or obstruction counts toward the primary full-project outcome only when it meets the scope announced for that brief. Record separately which result was obtained, which claimed stopping decisions independent assessors accepted, and which downstream costs the evidence supports. Report construction-only and obstruction-eligible briefs separately so different mixes of outcomes remain visible. Within the method pilot, method acceptance requires the predetermined accuracy, reliability and deployment-cost improvement on its withheld workload.

Use several participants and repeated seeds, with independent final assessment. Assess competence before assignment. Fix eligibility, allocation, model and harness versions, independent project count and the primary budget-accounting rule in advance. Pilot data can inform a precision or power analysis while the final evaluation stays reserved. On development briefs choose a machine adviser with strong performance alongside the lead. Allow different model families, private contexts, rival representations and computational tools. Freeze the pairing and the best unrestricted machine-only procedure before the fresh briefs arrive. Give every arm the same underlying evidence, access to tools and freedom to abandon a formulation. On matrix projects this includes the operator, grounding convention and tolerance. On arithmetic projects specify the target group, base field and required local or real behavior. Choosing a cover, branch data, or a different route belongs to the research team. Screen fresh briefs for known public answers before assignment. Assess any independently justified solution meeting the stated target, including a construction the assessors did not anticipate. A proof excluding one candidate family earns credit for that narrower result. Additional seeds measure variation within a project. Inference across projects draws its replication from independent projects.

Record person-hours, elapsed time, model expenditure, computation, checking, failed attempts and deployment costs separately. Report equal-time and equal-cost comparisons. Before combining costs, specify a human-hour price and the number of deployments over which discovery is amortized. Report sensitivity to declared alternative prices and horizons. A stop at one budget leaves success at larger budgets unknown. Fix any additional budgets in advance. Use the same economic rules in the specialist-alone arm when the stronger collaboration claim is tested. When a team uses a probe to choose its next step, record the decision it could change and the cost of obtaining the answer. Raiffa and Schlaifer give the formal comparison when the actions, beliefs and payoffs are specified. [122]

Evidence about choosing a partner

A weaker investigator can be the better partner. Minghao Liu and colleagues took an image classifier and changed its partner. The human answers were 81.9 percent accurate, the second classifier’s 90.0 percent. Yet at least one partner had the right answer on 95.8 percent of images in the mixed pair, against 92.3 percent for the two machines. Those ceilings assume someone always knows whom to trust. Handing the base model’s low-confidence cases to its partner also favored the human on these data. The human’s errors fell in different places. [126]

Different habits can also make partners worse. Jones and colleagues asked pairs to describe and identify the same abstract figures over five rounds. Two copies of GPT-5 reached 99 percent accuracy by round four. A human paired with GPT-5 reached 70 percent in round five. Shared habits helped two machines understand each other. On the images, the weaker partner covered errors the stronger one missed. In the naming game, the mixed pair struggled to make use of what each saw. A mathematician needs both abilities. Spot the overlooked distinction and express it in a form the other investigator can use. [126, 127]

In one SciPiVot case a 32-fold sweep in spring stiffness moved a simulated support’s deflection only from about 212 to 222 millimeters. All six runs hit the travel limit. The agent proposed changing the number of springs. The senior researcher asked how many springs carried the load and whether the limit had masked the response to stiffness. The two plans called for different experiments. Across 248 decisions from 26 projects, PhD students and postdocs new to the projects, with AI available, selected the senior researcher’s recorded correction in 73.4 percent of cases. The best tested model with the project record scored 63.5 percent. Both plans were supplied. Agreement with a retrospective judgment tells us who recognized the plan. On a live project somebody has to invent the second plan, run the test and decide what its answer means. [61]

What happens when someone has to choose the next experiment live? Savage and del Rio Chanona had six chemical-engineering postgraduates guide a Bayesian optimizer through a coiled-tube reactor design problem. They could choose starting designs and then pick among proposals over 32 iterations. The human-guided search found better designs early than the standard optimizer in 32 unassisted runs. The lead narrowed as evaluations accumulated. The test used a surrogate fitted to 75 flow simulations. The guided procedure had the early lead. It also started from different designs and offered a choice of proposals. On separate synthetic tests, choosing randomly from such a menu sometimes matched or beat the standard optimizer. Give the same menu and budget to an agent and a generalist on the reactor before attributing its lead to engineering judgment. [113]

Kasparov’s Advanced Chess gives the human-computer partnership a name and a challenge. Two amateurs won a 2005 freestyle tournament. They describe selecting promising moves, splitting the lines and cross-checking them with different engines. Their competitors had different equipment and preparation. Give a rival the procedure and the engines. What can the player still see first? [123]

Calling the human contribution “taste” explains too little. Yisong Yue locates mathematical taste in human experience while giving the Caltech-AIM machine a role in proposing conjectures, connections and strategies. Experience matters here if it changes which explanation gets a hearing. A specialist who has watched a fit hide a missing physical term has a reason to inspect the integral even when the residual looks excellent. An agent trained on different failures could acquire the same suspicion. Which partner follows it to a decisive check? [91, 101] Ding counted the attention to successor questions in 58 human papers and public AI accounts of eleven problems. The human texts gave follow-ups more space. A comparison of what authors chose to publish is evidence about exposition. After42 gave agents the opportunity to nominate questions and found that its shortlist reproduced expert consensus about as well as individual experts in one subfield. Neither study followed the competing selections to their mathematical consequences. [134, 143]

A second investigator can be a machine. On one problem in the second First Proof challenge, a critic that had followed the revisions accepted seven drafts of the same argument. A fresh critic rejected all seven. The referees found the submitted proof incomplete. On another problem the critic rejected every draft of a proof the referees accepted up to minor repairs. A critic can save a team from a bad proof. It can also cost the team a good one. [124]

In the same batch the human referees found a gap in a proof about valuated matroids. A proposed formula was supposed to produce a flag of valuated matroids. One citation established a special case. The other did not address the claim. The referees thought the formula likely to work, but the proof still owed its central step. A machine critic had accepted it. A fresh model reading the draft spotted the weak point and called it minor. After seeing the human solution it recognized how much work the proof still needed. The referees earned credit for judging the size of the gap from the submitted argument and its citations alone. [124]

Aletheia exposed a different limit of proof checking. An earlier version tackled 700 entries then listed as open in the Erdős problem database. It returned 212 possible solutions. The reviewing team judged 63 technically correct, 13 responsive to the intended question and four of those new full or partial solutions. Fifty technically correct answers missed their target. A critic could have compared the proposed statement with the original question before anybody spent time proving it. Wang and collaborators found a related failure in a different task. Across seven models, 60.6 percent of rewrites into a more abstract vocabulary widened the asserted domain. A faithful translation carries the restriction. A genuine generalization needs a proof. Who catches the difference before the team proves the wrong theorem? [3, 10]

Once a faithful statement and a candidate are in hand, a proof assistant can expose a particular unsolved obligation and a library can make trying lemmas cheap. Measure that advantage from the point where it actually begins. AlphaProof Nexus proved a real-valued version of a problem on Ben Green’s list. Green clarified that the functions in question were complex-valued. A numerical search supplied a candidate counterexample on the cyclic group of order three. The team formalized the new question and the agent verified the counterexample. A proof checker gave a decisive answer twice. Green knew which question he had meant. Give his clarification to every team. The test for a structural specialist is whether they can spot a new mismatch from evidence the agent also has. [115]

Testing the proposed diagnosis

Keep natural collaboration intact in the primary comparison. In the checkpoint study, elicit the lead’s, specialist’s and machine adviser’s diagnoses independently before revealing anyone else’s. Record which failures both machines miss, which a human also misses and which objections turn out to be false. In a randomized analyst study, fifty professionals followed the assigned agent preference on 86 percent of four choices among defensible alternatives. A person who sees the agent’s answer first may never put their independent judgment on the record. [74, 124]

Prespecify checkpoint selection and keep unsuccessful interventions. Freeze the evidence, method, agent context and learned state. Each adviser states the predicted failure, test and scope of repair before seeing the reserved outcomes. Conceal authorship and randomize repeated copies of the same lead to a human proposal, a machine proposal or no proposal. Use the same evaluator and remaining compute allowance. Charge for producing the advice. Record whether a correct objection was acted on and whether the repair worked. This distinguishes finding the failure from persuading a partner or implementing the fix. Full-project costs include reaching the checkpoint.

Classify cases before outcomes by the representation change, information in cheap diagnostics and cost of further experiments. The diagnostic hypothesis predicts a larger advantage over the machine adviser where the available signals fit several costly explanations and the agents tend to favor the same wrong one. Randomize access to the same additional diagnostic information across arms with a prespecified information control. Score intermediate predictions independently. A smaller specialist premium after disclosure is consistent with the mechanism. A success ceiling or information that compensates for implementation or orchestration can produce the same pattern. Prespecify checks for those alternatives in both portfolios.

Calibrate autonomous difficulty on separate development projects to assess floor and ceiling effects. Estimate the natural portfolio contrast first. In a separate analysis, match or stratify on novelty, available theory, ambiguity, and the cost and informativeness of feedback. Matching on ambiguity removes differences in its frequency. Unmeasured differences can still matter. After each round freeze the shared archive before releasing fresh cases, and keep their reserved results sealed until assessment is complete. Report the cost and downstream benefit of retrieval separately from the incremental benefit of live specialist help.

Decision rules

Declare δ as the minimum worthwhile increase in accepted discovery probability, distinct from the improvement required to accept a method. For both the human-partner-minus-machine-partner and specialist-minus-generalist contrasts, a lower confidence bound above δ supports a worthwhile gain. An upper bound below δ sets aside a gain of that size for the tested scope. An interval containing δ leaves it unresolved. If the specialist beats the machine partner but the generalist comparison excludes a worthwhile additional gain, report the benefit of assistance and the upper bound on specialization. [92]

To compare fields, let D(b) be the human partner’s gain over the machine partner in sparse computation minus the corresponding gain in structural mathematics at budget b. Declare a comparative margin η, zero for a directional claim or positive for a minimum worthwhile difference. A lower confidence bound above η supports that cross-portfolio contrast. An upper bound below η sets aside the declared difference for the tested scope. An interval containing η leaves the ranking unresolved. A claim that specialization itself contributes more in sparse computation also needs the cross-portfolio difference of the specialist-minus-generalist gains, with its own prespecified margin. Significance in one portfolio and an inconclusive result in the other give no such test.

Fix the sample size and stopping rule or use a prespecified sequential procedure with valid coverage. The joint confidence procedure must account for all planned contrasts, project pairing, repeated participants, repeated runs within projects and repeated inspection. Compare against the strongest frozen machine-only procedure before claiming a centaur advantage. Include both solo arms for a claim about beating both individual partners. New agent versions warrant fresh cases under stated application scopes and acceptance rules. Retain the results for the versions that produced them.

Comparing investigators on the mesh and sumset problems

Have outside collaborators supply fresh mesh and sumset problems before either team proposes a solution. Include other authors’ ruler families and meshes from another group. Give both teams equal access to whoever needs the result. On each brief run a specialist with one agent and two agents free to take different approaches. At the same error tolerance, did the specialist’s design need less storage on unseen meshes? On unseen addition tables, did it prove a tighter bound or cover a larger family at the same cost? Compare partners within each brief before comparing fields. Include generalists and solo specialists, and count failures and false vetoes. [89, 90, 105, 124]

Have analysts and additive combinatorialists each work on problems in both fields, with the order varied and a fresh case each time. Keep cases from an investigator’s own research line identifiable in the analysis. Let independent judges assess the results without team names. An advantage that follows people into their home territory points toward accumulated experience. A gain that recurs across specialists on problems with an unstable inverse points toward that operation. Either would tell us more than a comparison between my favorite theorem and somebody else’s solver. This experiment has yet to be run.

Hand both teams an unfamiliar discovery. Leave its successor unnamed. Have each propose a question, explain why either answer would matter and commit to what would count as progress. Then exchange anonymized proposals and give independent investigators equal resources to pursue them. Score the question before execution and the result afterward. Keep an arm in which each team pursues its own idea, so that the insight needed to carry it out can count too. A promising false conjecture that uncovers a sharp obstruction can beat a true one that changes nothing. Circling the sum-product conjecture in advance would give away the decision to look there.
